Learn Autonomous Driving with Phoebe

A warehouse row is a business event someone meant. A self-driving car writes frames from many sensors, each on its own clock: cheap to collect, expensive to label, and the cases that matter are the ones you almost never see. Safety is a rare-event claim over exposure, and the number most often quoted, disengagements, measures the operator's policy, not the car. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, runs through every session: zero fatal crashes in 150 million miles that do not yet prove anything, and a crash rate of 120.00 per 100 million that becomes 192.24 once it is matched to the roads people drive and the rare scenarios are counted, against a human 185.55. Six leader sessions on the decision, ten practitioner sessions on the build. The human rates are NHTSA's; every fleet number is constructed and computed in the page. Nothing here is investment advice or a view on any company.

If you come from a data team

Leader session 6 in three columns. What a conventional analyst, engineer or scientist already has that is worth more here, what is new, and what is actively dangerous.

Carries over

  • Joins and time semantics, pointed at sensor streams: an as-of join with a direction and a tolerance
  • Slicing a metric by segment, now by distance band, scenario and road context
  • Rates with a denominator, and refusing a count that has none
  • Reproducible pipelines: seeds, and a reference implementation that must agree to the digit

Must learn

  • Multi-sensor drive logs: frames, keyframes, and what each timestamp means
  • Label economics: labels are bought by the frame, and the rare frames cost most to find
  • Rare-event statistics: Poisson bounds over miles, the rule of three, exposure matching
  • Simulation and importance sampling for scenarios the fleet almost never meets

Must unlearn

  • "Nearest timestamp" as the default join: on one constructed second it is right for 28 of 72 frames
  • "No events means safe": zero deaths in 150 million miles still bounds the rate above 1.33
  • Comparing totals across different mixes: easy miles flatter a crash rate
  • Ranking by the easy metric: disengagements per mile picked the policy with 21 extra crashes

What is real here

Every number was computed before any page quoted it. The human baselines are real and cited; the fleet around them is constructed.

The baselinesNHTSA's 2022 crash summary: 1.33 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles, and 185.55 police-reported crashes per 100 million, derived from the same report. RAND's "Driving to Safety" method for the miles needed
The ladderZero fatal crashes in 150 million miles, bounded at 2.00; crashes 120.00 on the fleet's own mix, 147.74 matched, 192.24 with the rare scenarios added
The anti-lever and the breakScoring by disengagements picks a policy with 21 extra crashes. Dropping the rare-scenario slices turns 192.24 "worse" into 147.74 "proven". Both modelled and declared on the widget
The engine checks itselfSix properties on every page load, each shown to fail when its fault is planted back. A Python copy reproduces every bench number with zero difference
