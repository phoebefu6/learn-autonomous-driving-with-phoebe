The fleet feedback loop

A warehouse dashboard reads data that was going to arrive anyway. A fleet only learns from what it chooses to send home, and the software on the road makes that choice. Change the driving policy and you change the triggers that fire, the clips that upload, the scenarios that get mined and the evaluation that says the release was safe. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, runs the loop; its numbers come from the engine's canon run, and the reporting rules come from the NHTSA Standing General Order, read at source.

LiveThe loop in one paragraph3 min▶

The fleet drives and logs everything on the car (session 1). A small fraction comes home, chosen by triggers. The team mines it for rare scenarios (session 3), buys labels for the keyframes (session 2), curates for the tail (session 4), trains, and evaluates on slices and bounds (sessions 5, 7 and 8). The new policy rides in shadow, then takes control. Then it drives, and its own behaviour decides which triggers fire on the next turn.

Every stage of that loop has been taught already. What is new tonight is the loop itself: the output of the last stage is an input to the first, so a mistake does not stay where it was made.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Shadow mode. A candidate policy runs on the car's live inputs and logs what it would have done, without control of the vehicle.
  • Trigger. A rule on the car that decides which moments are uploaded: a takeover, a hard brake, a disagreement between policies.
  • Data flywheel. The loop from fleet logs to a new release and back to the fleet.
  • Drift. The miles a fleet drives stop looking like the miles it was evaluated on: a new city, a new season, a new mix of roads.
  • Standing General Order (SGO). NHTSA's crash reporting order for automated driving systems and Level 2 driver assistance.
  • Constructed. Lanehart's miles, policies and city B are generated or chosen for teaching; only the NHTSA rates and rules are real.
Part 1 · covers shadow mode and triggers

What the fleet chooses to send home

14 min live

Two mechanisms decide what a fleet learns from. Shadow mode lets a candidate policy watch the road and record its opinions without touching the wheel. Triggers decide which moments are worth the upload. Both feel like neutral plumbing, and neither is: shadow mode stops being evidence the moment the two policies disagree, and a trigger tied to the driving policy moves whenever the policy moves.

LiveWhat shadow mode logs, and what it cannot4 min▶

In shadow mode the candidate sees exactly the frames the deployed policy sees and writes down its plan. Nothing it decides reaches the steering or the brakes. That makes the comparison paired: same scene, same instant, two answers. Where they agree, the candidate inherits the deployed policy's record on those frames. Where they disagree, you have found the interesting moment, and a disagreement is a natural trigger for an upload.

What you cannot have is the next frame. The other road users reacted to the car that was actually driving. Session 6 met the same wall in log replay: once the new plan diverges from the logged path, the recording can no longer say what would have happened. Shadow mode tells you where to look, and hands the what next to simulation (session 6) and to rare-event statistics (sessions 7 and 8).

Real world

RAND's "Driving to Safety" (Kalra and Paddock 2016) concludes that developers cannot simply drive their way to safety: test driving alone cannot give enough evidence for fatalities and injuries, and other methods, including simulation, scenario testing and staged deployment, are needed. Shadow mode is one way of staging: the candidate earns miles of opinion before it earns miles of control.

Self-studyShadow mode is not an A/B test3 min▶

An A/B test randomises which version is in control and compares outcomes. Shadow mode never puts the candidate in control, so it measures agreement, not outcomes. The two answer different questions, and the mistake is to report a shadow disagreement rate as if it were a safety result. A low disagreement rate says the candidate drives like the old policy, which is reassuring only if the old policy was good on exactly those miles.

Randomised comparisons, power and sample size are owned by learn experimentation; they are not retaught here. The point on this side of the seam: on public roads the outcome you care about is rare enough that sessions 3 and 7 decide the design, not a dashboard.

LiveA trigger is a logging policy4 min▶

The canon run gives Lanehart 3,000 constructed conflicts in 150 million supervised miles, each with a severity between 0 and 1. A policy takes over when severity passes its threshold: cautious at 0.50, standard at 0.90, loose at 0.985. A conflict above 0.97 that nobody takes over crashes with probability 0.25. Suppose, as many teams do, that every takeover sends a clip home.

  • Cautious: 1,480 takeovers, 0.987 per 100,000 miles, 0 extra crashes. The mining queue is full of moments that were never dangerous.
  • Standard: 290 takeovers, 0.193 per 100,000 miles, 0 extra crashes. Five times fewer clips for identical safety.
  • Loose: 40 takeovers, 0.027 per 100,000 miles, 21 extra crashes, a true rate of 206.24 against 192.24. The conflicts that crashed are exactly the ones in the window between 0.97 and 0.985, and none of them fired the trigger.

So the trigger is not a sensor reading the road. It is the policy, reporting on itself. Score a release by "takeovers per mile" and the score picks loose; feed the loop from takeovers and the loop goes blind in the same place. Session 10's bench calls this the anti-lever.

Real world

California's regulation (13 CCR 227.50, as of the 2024 text) defines a disengagement as a deactivation of the autonomous mode when a failure of the technology is detected or when safe operation requires the test driver to take manual control, and asks who initiated it: the technology, the test driver, a remote operator or a passenger. Who decides to take over is the operator's policy, so a disengagement count measures the policy as much as the car. Whether disengagements per mile are a useful safety metric at all is contested; critics call the definition open to interpretation (reported).

Self-studyWriting a trigger you can audit3 min▶

A trigger has the same two errors as any classifier: it uploads moments that do not matter, and it misses moments that do. The first error costs storage and labelling budget (session 2). The second is invisible, because a moment that was never uploaded leaves no row to count.

The only way to see what triggers miss is to upload something no trigger chose: a small random sample of ordinary driving, kept on a fixed schedule that does not depend on the policy. It is the denominator for every trigger. Mine it with the same queries as session 3 and compare what the triggers sent with what the random sample says was out there. Keep at least one trigger that does not depend on the driving policy at all, such as a scenario query run on the car, so a policy change cannot switch it off.

Part 2 · covers the data flywheel, drift across cities and the 30-second reporting window

A loop that feeds itself, a road that changes, a rule that counts

11 min live

Put the stages in a ring and the danger shows: every turn changes the policy, and the policy changes what the next turn can see. Then move the fleet to a new city, and the same car is evaluated on a road mix it was never weighted for. Last, look at what the regulator's crash reporting actually counts, because that is the only part of the loop anyone outside the team sees.

LiveThe flywheel, stage by stage, and where it bends4 min▶

Read the ring as a set of filters. Step 2 decides which moments exist for everything after it. Step 3 can only mine what was uploaded. Step 4 only labels what was mined. Step 6 can only evaluate on the slices it kept. Every filter is reasonable alone, and their product is a picture of the road shaped by the last policy.

Two places bend the loop. The first is the yellow edge: a policy that takes over less uploads less, so the next training set holds fewer of the moments it handled badly. The second is step 6. On the canon run, Lanehart's exposure-matched crash rate is 147.74 per 100 million miles with an upper bound of 169.04, below the human 185.55. Add the importance-sampled tail (44.50 per 100M, session 8) and it becomes 192.24, upper 215.71: worse. Drop the four tail slices from evaluation and 147.74 reads as "proven". The loop then ships a release, the release changes the triggers, and the next turn has even less tail to evaluate on.

The fix is structural, not heroic: the tail slices and the random baseline sample from Part 1 sit outside the loop, so no policy change can remove them.

LiveDrift: the same car in a new city3 min▶

On the canon run Lanehart's crash rates by context are 69.09 on highway, 155.56 on urban day roads, 186.67 at night and 333.33 in rain, per 100 million miles. Its own mix of miles weighs them to 120.00. Weighted to the human mix (a constructed split), the same car is 147.74. Nothing about the software differs between those two numbers; only the road does. That is drift in its simplest form.

Now take city B, a constructed second city with a quarter of its miles in rain. The same rate card gives 187.37. Against the national 185.55 that looks worse; against humans weighted to city B's own mix, 216.01, it looks better. Neither answer is settled, because the human context rates are constructed multiples and rain, which now carries 44% of Lanehart's rate in city B, rests on 25 crashes in 7.5 million miles. Its exact 95% upper bound is 465.55 (derived from the canon counts in the build-along). The tail rates may move with the city too, and nothing in the log says by how much.

The canon already shows a thin mix flipping a verdict: at 60 million miles the matched rate is 168.23 with an upper bound of 205.62, not proven, because the rain and night miles are too thin. A new city is that problem arriving all at once.

LiveWhat the 30-second window captures, and what it never will4 min▶

The Standing General Order, read at source, makes an ADS crash (SAE Levels 3 to 5) reportable if the system was in use at any time within 30 seconds of the crash and the crash met the damage or injury criteria. The window is there for case B: a system that hands control to a person a few seconds before impact cannot move the crash off its record by handing over. Level 2 driver assistance has narrower criteria: a vulnerable road user struck, a fatality, an air bag deployment or hospital transport. In NHTSA's words, crash reporting criteria are stricter for ADS.

Three things never pass through it. Miles: reporting entities are not required to report vehicles operated or distances traveled. Near misses: case D is exactly the conflict a fleet trigger should upload, and it leaves no report. And anything outside the window, like case C. So the public record of the loop is a numerator with no denominator, while the fleet's own triggers hold far more, and the fleet chooses them.

Real world

NHTSA's SGO page states that data on the number of crashes reported have not been normalized or adjusted by any measure of exposure, including operational design domains or vehicle miles traveled. It also lists limits: reports may be incomplete or unverified, the same crash may have several reports, ADS and Level 2 were sometimes misclassified in earlier versions, and telemetry differs by entity. The Order was first issued in 2021 and amended in 2021, 2023 and 2025.

Self-studyThe comparability disagreement, taught both ways3 min▶

One side: a fleet with full telemetry records crashes no police officer would ever see, so its crash count is inflated against police-reported human crashes (NHTSA's 2022 figure is about 5.93 million police-reported crashes, 185.55 per 100 million miles derived), and the fleet looks worse than it is. RAND itself keeps separate "reported" and "estimated total" human rates for this reason.

The other side: the fleet chooses its triggers, its takeover policy and, under the 30-second rule, part of what counts as its crash, so a count can also be shaped downward. Both effects are real and neither has a settled size. Leader session 4 and session 10 show the question to ask instead of picking a side: on which miles, matched to which exposure, with which tail included?

Part 3 · build-along

Re-weight the fleet for a new city, and filter by the window

17 min hands on

Rebuild the drift figure in pandas from the four canon contexts, put an exact bound on each context, then write the 30-second rule as a filter. Run it in a notebook or a scratch folder with pandas and scipy. The figure is the plan: two small tables, one weighted sum done on both sides, and one bound that tells you how much to trust the slice that drift made heavy.

Make the contexts table from the canon run

Four road contexts, Lanehart's crash counts and miles from the canon run (150 million constructed miles), and the human context rates to two decimals. The rates should come out at 69.09, 155.56, 186.67 and 333.33.

pythonimport pandas as pd ctx = pd.DataFrame({ "context": ["highway", "urban", "night", "rain"], "k": [57, 70, 28, 25], "miles": [82.5e6, 45e6, 15e6, 7.5e6], "human": [92.31, 212.32, 258.48, 295.40], }).set_index("context") ctx["rate"] = ctx.k / ctx.miles * 1e8 ctx.round(2)

Weight both sides by three mixes

Lanehart's own mix, the constructed human mix, and city B. You should see 120.00, 147.74 and 187.37 on Lanehart's side, and 185.55 and 216.01 on the human side for the last two. The own-mix human figure prints 155.08 here, against 155.09 in the figure, because the inputs are rounded to two decimals; that gap is a useful reminder of how much rounding the engine avoids.

pythonmixes = pd.DataFrame({ "own": [0.55, 0.30, 0.10, 0.05], "human_mix": [0.35, 0.40, 0.15, 0.10], "city_b": [0.20, 0.40, 0.15, 0.25], }, index=ctx.index) side = pd.DataFrame({"lanehart": mixes.T @ ctx.rate, "humans": mixes.T @ ctx.human}).round(2) side

Put an exact bound on every context

The exact Poisson upper bound from session 7, per context. Rain comes out at 465.55 against a point of 333.33; highway, with 57 crashes on 82.5 million miles, only reaches 86.14. Then ask how much of city B's rate each context carries: rain is 44%.

pythonfrom scipy.stats import chi2 ctx["upper"] = chi2.ppf(0.95, 2 * ctx.k + 2) / 2 / ctx.miles * 1e8 share = mixes.city_b * ctx.rate / (mixes.city_b @ ctx.rate) pd.DataFrame({"upper": ctx.upper, "share_city_b": share}).round(2)

Try your own city

Change the city B column to a mix you know: more night, less highway, whatever your city drives. Your numbers will differ. Before you read the result, write down which context you expect to dominate, then check the share column. The surprise is almost always the thinnest context.

Write the 30-second rule as a filter

Four illustrative cases, the same as the timeline figure. A case counts as an ADS crash report if there was a crash and the system was last in use 30 seconds or less before it. You should get A and B only. Add a column for the damage or injury criteria and the filter needs it too; the near miss never enters at all.

pythonevents = pd.DataFrame({ "case": ["A", "B", "C", "D"], "crash": [True, True, True, False], "ads_last_in_use_s": [0, 12, 45, 0], }) events["sgo_ads_report"] = events.crash & (events.ads_last_in_use_s <= 30) events

Write down what each filter cannot see

One line each: the trigger (what it misses), the 30-second rule (miles, near misses, older handovers), and the city mix (which context is thin). That list belongs in the release review, next to the rate.

The habit to keep. Every time the fleet changes, the policy, the triggers or the city, ask what the data pipeline can no longer see. Keep a random sample and the tail slices outside the loop so no release can switch them off. Re-weight to the new road mix before you compare, and put an exact bound on whichever context the new mix made heavy.

Check yourself

Three questions

A candidate policy runs in shadow mode for a month and disagrees with the deployed policy on a small share of frames. What does that tell you?

Shadow mode is paired on the same frames, which makes disagreements easy to find. After a disagreement, the road reacted to the policy in control, so the candidate's outcome is a counterfactual for simulation and rare-event statistics, not a measurement.

On the canon run, the loose policy has 40 takeovers against standard's 290, and every takeover uploads a clip. What happened to the loop?

A takeover trigger reports on the policy. Loose stops taking over in the window from 0.97 to 0.985, which is exactly where untaken conflicts crash, so the loop goes blind where the car got worse (206.24 against 192.24).

The count of ADS crash reports under the Standing General Order rises year on year. What can you conclude?

NHTSA says the counts have not been normalized or adjusted by any measure of exposure. A rise could be more miles, more fleets, or more complete telemetry. The 30-second window exists so that a late handover does not remove a crash from the record.
