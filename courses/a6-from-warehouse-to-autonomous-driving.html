Part 0

Lanehart, six weeks after the first drive logs landed

Lanehart is a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city. The analyst who arrived from a retail warehouse in leader session 1 has now been asked a different question by her manager: "what do you still need to learn, and what should we stop doing?" She could answer with a reading list. A reading list does not say which of her habits will quietly produce a wrong number. A transfer map does, because it puts every skill in one of three columns and attaches the evidence that put it there.

Live = presented in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. A three-column transfer map, CARRIES OVER, MUST LEARN, MUST UNLEARN, with four concrete cards in each column and the session that owns each card. Two of the unlearn cards drawn as the mechanism that makes the old habit wrong. A 90-day self-study ladder of free sources, every one marked official or not, with the paid standards named honestly as paid. And your own transfer card, sorted through two tests, ready to show a manager.

LiveWhy a map, not a reading list3 min▶

Someone moving from a warehouse into driving data does not arrive empty. They bring joins, slices, rates and reproducible pipelines, and most of that is worth more here than it was before. The risk is not ignorance. The risk is a habit that worked for years and now returns a confident wrong answer with no error message.

So the map has three columns, not two. "Must learn" is the usual training plan. "Must unlearn" is the column most plans leave out, and it is the one that protects the safety claim. Each unlearn card carries a number from Lanehart's constructed bench, so the case against the old habit is shown, not asserted.

Real world

The regulator's own crash-reporting page is the best argument that the warehouse's rate discipline carries over. NHTSA's Standing General Order page says the crash counts "have not" been "normalized or adjusted by any measure of exposure, including operational design domains or vehicle miles traveled". An analyst who refuses to compare counts without a denominator already has the instinct this field needs most. Leader session 4 is built on that sentence.

Self-studyHow to read a card on the map3 min▶
  • Skill. What you can do, in plain words: join on time, slice a metric, bound a rate.
  • Method. The specific technique that skill turns into here: an as-of join with a direction, an exact Poisson bound, an exposure-matched rate.
  • Tool class. The kind of tool, never a product: a dataframe as-of merge, a grouped metric table, a seeded generator, an annotation cost model.
  • Reading. One source that teaches it, and the session here that practises it (a1 to a6 for leaders, p1 to p10 for practitioners).
  • Constructed. Every Lanehart number on the unlearn side comes from the seeded bench. The human baselines (1.33 deaths and 185.55 police-reported crashes per 100 million miles, US 2022) are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers the three columns: carries over, must learn, must unlearn

Twelve cards, three columns, one session each

15 min live

The map agrees with the "If you come from a data team" block on the landing page, card for card, and adds what the block has no room for: the method, the tool class and the reading behind each one. Read it left to right. The left column is what you re-point, the middle is what you add, and the right is what you must catch yourself doing.

LiveCarries over: four skills, re-pointed4 min▶

Joins and time semantics. You already know an as-of join: "the price in force when the order was placed". Here the same join attaches each camera frame to a lidar scan, and the direction matters. In the public multi-sensor research dataset read for practitioner session 1, a camera fires while the lidar sweeps past it and the lidar scan is stamped when its rotation completes, so the right scan is the one stamped after the frame. Tool class: a dataframe as-of merge with an explicit direction and tolerance.

Slicing a metric by segment. You sliced revenue by region and channel. Now slice recall by distance band and by scenario, because a detector's misses cluster far away, half hidden, at night. Tool class: a grouped metric table. Practitioner session 5.

Rates with a denominator. You refused a count with no base. The regulator's crash page agrees with you in writing. Leader session 4.

Reproducible pipelines. Seeds, versioned inputs, and a reference implementation that must agree to the digit. Lanehart's bench is checked this way: the shipped engine and an independent reference agree on 881 values, and planting a 0.1% fault on one value fails the comparison. Practitioner session 10.

LiveMust learn: four things nobody hands over4 min▶

Multi-sensor drive logs. Frames from six cameras at 12 Hz, a lidar at 20 Hz and five radars at 13 Hz in the dataset paper practitioner session 1 reads, each with its own meaning of "timestamp". Reading: Caesar et al. (2020), the nuScenes paper.

Label economics. Labels are bought by the frame. The same paper labels keyframes at 2 Hz and reports that this holds up when interpolated to 10 to 20 Hz. The rare frames cost most, because you pay to find them before you pay to label them. Tool class: an annotation cost model. Practitioner session 2.

Rare-event statistics. Exact Poisson bounds over miles, the rule of three, and exposure matching. Reading: Kalra and Paddock (2016), RAND RR-1478, whose Table 1 needs 275 million failure-free miles to show a fatality rate below 1.09 per 100 million at 95% confidence. Practitioner session 7.

Simulation and importance sampling. For scenarios the fleet almost never meets, skew the simulator toward risk and reweight the result back to real-world rates. Reading: Zhao et al. (2017); the authors report 2,000 to 20,000 times acceleration in lane-change scenarios. On Lanehart's constructed bench, importance sampling is 5.2 times tighter than plain Monte Carlo at the same budget (SD 1.404 against 7.251). Practitioner session 8.

LiveMust unlearn: four habits, each with its number4 min▶

"Nearest timestamp" as the default join. On one constructed second of Lanehart's timing model, joining 72 camera frames to the nearest lidar stamp puts 28 on the right scan and 44 on the wrong one, with no error raised. Joining to the first scan stamped strictly after the frame puts all 72 right.

"No events means safe." Lanehart's pilot reports zero fatal crashes in 150 million constructed miles. The exact 95% upper bound is still 2.00 per 100 million, above the human 1.33. With zero events the bound clears 1.33 only after 225.2 million miles.

Comparing totals across different mixes. On its own mix of mostly highway miles, Lanehart's crash rate is 120.00 per 100 million. Re-weighted to the human driving mix, it is 147.74. At 60 million miles the exposure match flips the verdict from proven to not proven, because the thin night and rain miles carry the weight.

Ranking by the easy metric. Score three takeover policies by disengagements per mile and the loose policy wins with 0.027 per 100,000 miles. It also lets through 21 extra crashes, 14.00 per 100 million worse. The cautious policy has five times the disengagements of the standard one and identical safety. Disengagements measure the policy as much as the car.

Two of those unlearn cards are worth drawing as the mechanism, because the mechanism is what convinces a team, not the number alone.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree, and the map stays honest4 min▶

Is a fleet's crash count comparable with police-reported human crashes? NHTSA's 2022 summary counts about 5.93 million police-reported crashes, which works out to 185.55 per 100 million miles. Police reports undercount minor crashes. A fleet with full telemetry records more of its own minor crashes than a human population reports. Some argue that makes fleet rates look worse than they are; others argue the matching problem cuts both ways. The map puts "rates with a denominator" in carries over, and leaves the comparability question open.

Is disengagement a useful metric at all? California's regulation, 13 CCR 227.50 as of the 2024 text, defines a disengagement and requires annual reports of autonomous miles and who initiated each one. Critics call the definition open to interpretation (reported). The map does not say "never count disengagements"; it says never rank by them alone.

When will Level 4 be broadly available? Open, and the people closest to the work disagree. No card on the map depends on a date.

Part 2 · covers the 90-day self-study ladder, free sources only

Five rungs, each marked official or not

10 min live

Every rung is free to read, and each says plainly whether it is official, meaning issued by a regulator or a standards body, or not. The order is deliberate: definitions first, then what the regulator counts, then the arithmetic of proof, then the shape of the data, then the method for the tail. The three paid standards sit beside the ladder, named honestly as paid.

LiveDays 1 to 30: the official rungs4 min▶

Days 1 to 14. SAE J3016 visual chart (official, free). SAE International made J3016_202104 free and publishes the chart for distribution with attribution. Read it until you can say, for any level, who is driving. Pair it with NHTSA's "Summary of Motor Vehicle Traffic Crashes: 2022 Data", DOT HS 813 643 (official, free): 42,514 people killed, 1.33 per 100 million vehicle miles, 41% of deaths on rural roads that carry 32% of the miles. Leader sessions 1 and 4 replay those pages.

Days 15 to 30. NHTSA Standing General Order page and its data (official, free). Learn what triggers a report (an automated driving system in use within 30 seconds of a crash), what the page says the counts are not, and the limitations it lists: incomplete reports, duplicates, earlier misclassification. Then California Code of Regulations, Title 13, section 227.50 (official, free), the disengagement reporting rule, read as of the 2024 text, because a 2025 proposal to amend it was not confirmed as adopted when the source map was checked.

Real world

California's rule defines a disengagement as a deactivation of the autonomous mode when a failure of the technology is detected, or when safe operation requires the test driver to take manual control. The annual report must say who initiated it: the technology, the test driver, a remote operator or a passenger. That "who" is the point of rung two: a takeover is a decision made under a policy, so a count of them measures the policy as much as the car.

LiveDays 31 to 50: the arithmetic of proof3 min▶

Kalra and Paddock (2016), "Driving to Safety", RAND RR-1478 (free research report, not official). Independent research, free to read, and the most cited argument in this field for why driving alone cannot prove safety. Work through its three questions with the numbers in Table 1: 275 million failure-free miles to show a fatality rate below 1.09 per 100 million at 95% confidence; about 8.8 billion miles to estimate the rate within 20%; 11 billion miles to show it is 20% better with 80% power. The report's own conclusion is that developers "cannot simply drive" their way to safety.

Then redo the first number with the 2022 rate from rung one. At 1.33 it is 225.2 million miles. Why it fell is the lesson: the human rate rose.

Self-studyDays 51 to 90: the data and the method for the tail5 min▶

Days 51 to 70. Dataset papers (free, not official). Read them as descriptions of how a drive log is built, not as recommendations of any dataset. Start with Caesar et al. (2020), "nuScenes: A multimodal dataset for autonomous driving", arXiv 1903.11027: its synchronisation section is practitioner session 1 in the authors' own words. Its data licence is non-commercial. Then, for comparison of design choices, the papers behind three more public datasets: Geiger, Lenz and Urtasun (2012), the KITTI vision benchmark suite; Sun et al. (2020), Waymo Open Dataset; Chang et al. (2019), Argoverse. These three are named here, not read at source for this page, so check each paper yourself.

Days 71 to 90. Zhao et al. (2017), arXiv 1605.04965 (free preprint, not official). The published version is IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Transportation Systems 18(3):595-607. Cut-ins modelled from naturalistic driving data, skewed toward riskier behaviour, then reweighted to real-world rates. The authors report 2,000 to 20,000 times acceleration. Practitioner session 8 runs the same idea on Lanehart's constructed tail.

Paid, beside the ladder. UL 4600 (Edition 3, 2023) describes a safety case as structured claims, argument and evidence across the lifecycle. ISO 26262 covers hazards from malfunctions of electrical and electronic systems; ISO 21448 (safety of the intended functionality) covers hazards with no fault at all, such as a sensor that works as designed and still misses. All three are paid standards, so they appear here as concepts at the reported tier only. Read their public summaries before deciding whether your team needs to buy them.

Part 3 · build-along

Sort your own skills into the three columns

17 min hands on

No code. Write your own transfer card, the one you would show a manager. Every skill you bring goes through two tests. The tests are the two ways the warehouse playbook breaks hardest: events that almost never happen, and a source that is many sensors on their own clocks instead of a row someone meant.

Write ten skills you actually use

From your last warehouse role, in your own words: "as-of joins on price history", "weekly churn by segment", "A/B test readouts", "tested data models". One per line. Do not pre-sort.

Run test one: does it still hold when the event is rare?

A weekly count of a common event fails it; a rate with a denominator and an exact bound passes. If it fails, move it to MUST UNLEARN and write the number that shows why, borrowing from the map: "0 deaths in 150 million miles still bounds at 2.00" (constructed).

Run test two: does it still hold with many sensors on their own clocks?

Anything that assumes one row per event someone meant fails it. A join habit that defaults to "nearest" fails it; the same join with a direction and a tolerance passes. Write the re-pointed version next to it.

Name the seat for each survivor

Drive logs, labels, evaluation slices, simulation or the safety claim. Add the session that practises it, from the codes on the map. A skill with no seat is not wrong, it is just not part of this job.

Find the empty seats, then pick rungs

Any seat nobody on your team has worked in goes to MUST LEARN. Give each one a rung of the ladder and a date inside the 90 days. Mark which of those sources are official.

Compare with the landing page's three columns

If your MUST UNLEARN column is empty, look again at the four habits on the map. An empty unlearn column is the commonest sign that the sort was done too kindly.

The habit to keep. Every time you reach for a familiar technique on driving data, ask the two questions first: does it still hold when the event is rare, and does it still hold with many clocks? If either answer is no, the technique is not wrong, it is pointed at the wrong thing. Re-point it before you run it.

Check yourself

Three questions

A new hire joins each camera frame to the lidar scan with the nearest timestamp, the way they joined prices to orders. Where does that habit go on the transfer map?

The as-of join itself carries over; the default direction must be unlearned. The lidar scan is stamped when its rotation completes, so a camera that fires mid-sweep belongs to the scan stamped after it. Practitioner session 1 runs the join.

Which of these sources on the 90-day ladder is official, in the sense of being issued by a regulator or a standards body?

The California regulation is official and free. RAND RR-1478 is a free independent research report, and the Zhao et al. preprint is free academic work; both are valuable and neither is official. UL 4600 and the ISO standards are paid and sit beside the ladder.

A dashboard ranks takeover policies by fewest disengagements per mile. Where does that practice sit on the map?

A takeover is a decision made under a policy, and California's rule asks who initiated each one for that reason. The cautious policy had five times the disengagements of the standard one and identical safety. Rank by an outcome, not by the policy's own trace.

Before practitioner session 1

Homework

Thirty minutes this week, then a ten-minute check at day 30.

Climb rung one

Read the SAE J3016 chart and the first pages of NHTSA DOT HS 813 643. Write the level definitions from memory, then the 2022 rate and the rural share of deaths and miles. Check yourself against the sources.

Show your transfer card to one person

A manager, a mentor or a colleague on the perception team. Ask them which card in your MUST UNLEARN column they would have missed. Add one card if they name a habit you left out.

Book the day-30 re-sort

Put a calendar entry for day 30: sort the same ten skills again after rung two. Skills that move columns are the evidence the ladder is working.
