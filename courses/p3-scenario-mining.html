Scenario mining: querying the fleet for rare events

The scenarios that decide whether a car is safe are rare, and nobody can watch every mile to find them. So they are found by a query: filters over the actor tracks a drive log already holds, the type of road user, how far away, whether something blocked the view, how many seconds until contact. A query returns a set, and that set has a precision you can measure and a recall you mostly cannot. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies a 24-event drive-log table, constructed for this page, and every count is computed in your browser.

Part 0

Lanehart's request: "find me every pedestrian stepping out from behind a van"

Lanehart is a constructed robotaxi pilot. Its safety team holds a list of rare scenario classes, and the first one is a pedestrian who steps out from behind a parked van. In Lanehart's constructed model that encounter happens about 4,000 times per 100 million miles, and the car fails on about 3 in every 1,000 of them. Nobody can watch 100 million miles to find 4,000 moments. The analyst writes a query instead, and the question for tonight is what came back, and what did not.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A scenario query written as four filters (actor type, distance, occlusion, time to collision) over a drive-log table. The habit of reading its result as a mined set with two numbers attached: precision, the share of returned clips that really are the scenario, and recall, the share of real scenarios the query found. On Lanehart's constructed 24-event table, the four default filters return 5 events, 3 of them real: precision 60%, recall 3 of 6. And the uncomfortable rule behind it: the clips a query never returns are the clips nobody ever reviews.

LiveWhy mine, instead of drive and watch4 min▶

The arithmetic of the tail. Session a2 drew it: a rare scenario's crash rate is how often you meet it times how often you fail when you do. Lanehart's four constructed classes add up to 46 per 100 million miles. The occluded pedestrian alone is 4,000 encounters times 0.003 failures, 12 per 100 million.

What mining looks for. Not the failure, the encounter. At 4,000 against 12 per 100 million miles, the encounter is about 333 times more common (derived), so it is the thing a query can find often enough to train on, test on and simulate from. Session 8 uses exactly these encounters to drive importance sampling.

What the log offers. A drive log already holds tracks of every road user the car perceived: type, position, speed, and attributes such as "partly hidden". A scenario is a pattern over those tracks, so it can be written as a filter.

Real world

The public multi-sensor research dataset from session 1 annotates 23 object classes and 8 attributes on keyframes sampled at 2 Hz, with 3D boxes giving position, size and heading. Those are exactly the columns a scenario filter runs on, and the 2 Hz rate is a warning: a filter on human labels sees only keyframes, while a filter on every frame runs on what the car's own perception inferred.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Scenario. A pattern of road users and context the car has to handle, such as a pedestrian appearing from behind a parked vehicle.
  • Event. One candidate moment in the log, around one actor track.
  • Occlusion. Something blocks the sensors' view of the actor, wholly or partly.
  • Time to collision (TTC). Seconds until contact if neither the car nor the actor changes speed or path: distance divided by closing speed.
  • Mined set. The events a query returns.
  • Precision. Of the events returned, the share that really are the scenario.
  • Recall. Of the real scenarios in the log, the share the query returned.
  • Constructed. Lanehart's 24 events are written for this page, not recorded.
Part 1 · covers a scenario query as filters

Four filters turn a description into a query

15 min live

"A pedestrian stepping out from behind a van" is a sentence. The query is that sentence translated into columns: the actor is a pedestrian, the track was occluded, it was close, and contact was seconds away. Each filter narrows the pile. The figure follows Lanehart's constructed table of 24 events, 6 of which a reviewer confirmed as the real scenario.

LiveWhat each filter is really asking6 min▶
  • Actor type. A class from perception or from a label. Cheap and sharp, and as wrong as the classifier is: a pedestrian pushing a bicycle may be logged as a cyclist.
  • Occlusion. Was the actor hidden for part of the track? This is the filter that carries the scenario's meaning ("from behind a van"), and it is itself an inference. On Lanehart's table, event e05 is a real step-out whose occlusion flag was never set, so no query that requires the flag can find it.
  • Distance. How close the actor came. Distance is what makes a step-out dangerous, but a fixed cut-off is a guess: e06 is a real step-out at 34 m on a faster road, and "within 30 m" drops it.
  • Time to collision. Distance over closing speed. It folds speed into distance, which is why it usually beats either alone, but it assumes nobody changes course. A reviewer looking at e04 (3.4 s) still called it the scenario.

None of the four filters is wrong. Each one is a definition, and each definition has an edge where real scenarios fall off.

LiveQuery, not detector4 min▶

A detector learns what "unusual" looks like and flags whatever surprises it. A scenario query starts from a named class someone wrote down, and returns what matches the definition. Mining by query is the right tool when the safety case lists the scenario in advance, as Lanehart's four classes do: the result can be counted, reviewed and rerun on next month's miles with the same meaning.

Generic detectors (isolation forests, statistical thresholds) belong to learn anomaly detection. They are good at finding the scenario nobody listed yet; they are a poor way to count one that is listed.

Self-studyWhere the columns come from5 min▶

Every column in a scenario query is produced upstream. Actor type and occlusion come from perception or from labels; distance comes from the box positions, which depend on the sensor sync from session 1; time to collision is computed from two tracks over several frames. A query is therefore only as good as the pipeline under it.

That gives a practical rule: when a mined set looks thin, check the columns before the thresholds. A missing occlusion flag (e05) cannot be fixed by loosening the distance.

The mined set has a precision, and the misses have no row

LiveRead the widget three ways5 min▶
  • Defaults (pedestrian, occluded, 30 m, 3 s): 5 returned, 3 real. Precision 60%, recall 3 of 6. Two look-alikes to review, three real step-outs lost.
  • Loosen to 40 m and 4 s: 7 returned, 5 real. Precision 71%, recall 5 of 6. Both numbers went up. The first thresholds were set on instinct, and instinct is not a reason to believe a tighter filter is a better one.
  • Drop the occlusion requirement: e05 can now come back, along with look-alikes that were never hidden. Watch which way precision moves at your settings.

What no setting fixes: the hidden column on the widget is the reviewer's verdict. On a real log that column does not exist for the clips the query skipped.

LiveMeasuring each number4 min▶

Precision is cheap. Review a random sample of the mined set and count the real ones. It also sets the review bill: at 60% precision a reviewer opens about 1.7 clips per real scenario, at 25% four.

Recall is expensive. To estimate it, review a random sample of clips the query did NOT return and count real scenarios hiding there. Because the scenario is rare, that sample is mostly empty, and an empty sample is still a result: with 0 found in n reviewed clips, the 95% upper bound on the miss rate per clip is about 3 / n, the rule of three (reported, Hanley and Lippman-Hand 1983). Reviewing 300 skipped clips and finding none bounds the miss rate below about 1% per clip (derived). Session 7 does the exact version of that bound.

Real world

RAND's "Driving to Safety" (Kalra and Paddock 2016) concludes that developers cannot simply drive their way to safety, and names scenario testing and simulation among the other methods needed. Scenario testing needs scenarios, and a mined set is where they come from. If the query's recall is unknown, the test library inherits that blind spot: the scenarios it never returned are never tested.

Self-studyWhy recall matters more for evaluation than for training4 min▶

For training, a mined set with modest recall can still be useful: 300 real step-outs teach a model something even if 300 more were missed. For evaluation, recall is the whole question. If the step-outs the query misses are systematically different (faster roads, flags that perception failed to set), then a model scored on the mined set is scored on the easy half of the scenario.

That is the bridge to session 4: what goes in a training mix and what must stay in the evaluation slices. General precision and recall as metrics are owned by learn model evaluation; here they describe a query, not a model.

Part 3 · build-along

Write the scenario query in pandas

17 min hands on

Rebuild Lanehart's 24-event table in pandas, run the four filters, and get the widget's numbers. Then do the part that matters on a real log: list who you missed, and plan the audit that would find them. The figure is the plan.

Make the constructed log

Twenty-four candidate events, one row each: actor type, closest distance in metres, an occlusion flag, time to collision in seconds, and the reviewer's verdict in truth. The verdict column is the luxury a real log does not have; it is here so you can see what the query misses.

pythonimport pandas as pd rows = [ ("e01", "pedestrian", 12, 1, 1.4, 1), ("e02", "pedestrian", 18, 1, 1.9, 1), ("e03", "pedestrian", 25, 1, 2.6, 1), ("e04", "pedestrian", 22, 1, 3.4, 1), ("e05", "pedestrian", 15, 0, 1.7, 1), ("e06", "pedestrian", 34, 1, 2.2, 1), ("e07", "pedestrian", 9, 1, 1.2, 0), ("e08", "pedestrian", 28, 1, 2.8, 0), ("e09", "pedestrian", 16, 1, 9.0, 0), ("e10", "pedestrian", 14, 0, 1.5, 0), ("e11", "pedestrian", 40, 0, 6.0, 0), ("e12", "pedestrian", 11, 0, 8.5, 0), ("e13", "pedestrian", 30, 1, 4.5, 0), ("e14", "pedestrian", 19, 0, 2.4, 0), ("e15", "cyclist", 13, 1, 1.6, 0), ("e16", "cyclist", 24, 1, 2.9, 0), ("e17", "cyclist", 35, 0, 5.0, 0), ("e18", "vehicle", 20, 1, 1.8, 0), ("e19", "vehicle", 45, 0, 3.0, 0), ("e20", "vehicle", 8, 0, 0.9, 0), ("e21", "vehicle", 60, 1, 7.0, 0), ("e22", "vehicle", 27, 0, 2.1, 0), ("e23", "vehicle", 33, 1, 3.8, 0), ("e24", "vehicle", 50, 0, 9.5, 0), ] log = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["event", "actor", "dist_m", "occluded", "ttc_s", "truth"]) log.truth.sum()

Write the query as one mask

Four conditions, joined with &, each in its own brackets. You should get 5 rows, 3 of them real: the widget's default.

pythonq = ((log.actor == "pedestrian") & (log.occluded == 1) & (log.dist_m <= 30) & (log.ttc_s <= 3)) mined = log[q] len(mined), mined.truth.sum(), mined.truth.mean()

Print the funnel

Add one filter at a time and print rows kept and real kept: 24 and 6, 14 and 6, 9 and 5, 8 and 4, 5 and 3. This is figure 1, and it is the table to show anyone who asks why the mined set is so small.

pythonstages = [("pedestrian", log.actor == "pedestrian"), ("occluded", log.occluded == 1), ("within 30 m", log.dist_m <= 30), ("ttc <= 3 s", log.ttc_s <= 3)] m = pd.Series(True, index=log.index) for name, cond in stages: m &= cond print(f"{name:12} kept {m.sum():2} real {log.truth[m].sum()}")

List who you missed

Real scenarios outside the mask: e04 (TTC 3.4 s), e05 (occlusion flag never set) and e06 (34 m). Each one has a different cause, and only two of the three are a threshold.

pythonlog[(log.truth == 1) & ~q]

Break it on purpose: or instead of and

Replace every & in the mask with |. The query now returns 22 of 24 events, all 6 real ones among them: recall is perfect and precision is 27%. A mined set that big is not a query, it is the log again. Put the & back.

pythonq_or = ((log.actor == "pedestrian") | (log.occluded == 1) | (log.dist_m <= 30) | (log.ttc_s <= 3)) len(log[q_or]), log[q_or].truth.sum()

Plan the recall audit

Write one line: how many skipped clips you would review at random, and what bound an empty result buys you (about 3 / n at 95%). On a real log this sample is drawn from millions of skipped clips, not 19.

The habit to keep. Ship every scenario query with three things attached: the filter definitions in plain words, the precision from a reviewed sample of what it returned, and either a recall audit of what it skipped or the sentence "recall not measured". A mined set without that third line looks complete and is not.

Check yourself

Three questions

On Lanehart's constructed table, tightening the query from four filters at 40 m and 4 s to 30 m and 3 s moves precision from 71% to 60% and recall from 5 of 6 to 3 of 6. What does that show?

The 30 m cut dropped a real step-out at 34 m, and the 3 s cut dropped one at 3.4 s, while the look-alikes stayed. Whether a filter helps is an empirical question about the rows it removes.

Event e05 is a real step-out whose occlusion flag was never set. Which change finds it?

A query inherits the misses of the pipeline that made its columns. When a real scenario is lost on a flag, the fix is upstream (or a looser definition), never a different threshold.

A team reports "our mined set is 95% precise". What is missing before anyone should trust it as a test library?

Precision is measured on what came back; it says nothing about what did not. The or-query in the build-along has perfect recall and 27% precision, so size alone is not the fix either.
