Learn Autonomous Driving with Phoebe · Leader session 1 of 6

The car is a data pipeline on wheels

A warehouse row is a business event someone meant. A self-driving car writes something else: frames from cameras, lidar and radar, each on its own clock, turned into guesses about what is out there, where it will go and what to do about it. Those frames are cheap to collect and expensive to label, and the moments that matter are the ones you almost never see. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, is the case for all sixteen sessions; every Lanehart number is generated, not observed. Tonight is the pipeline, where the data team sits in it, and what an SAE level does and does not tell you. Nothing here is investment advice.

Part 0

Lanehart, on the morning the first drive logs land

Lanehart is a constructed robotaxi pilot: a small fleet driving a fictional city, a safety lead, a perception team, and a data analyst who came from a retail warehouse. Her first ticket says "build the safety dashboard". The logs that arrive are not a fact table. They are hours of sensor recordings, a few labelled moments, and a fleet that has never once done the thing everyone most wants to know about. The six leader sessions are about reading that pile as a decision-maker; the ten practitioner sessions are about building on it.

LiveWhy the warehouse instinct misfires here4 min▶

A warehouse analyst trusts three things without naming them. A row has an author: an order was placed, an invoice raised. A row has an identity: a customer id somebody can be asked about. And the events that matter are common enough to count every week: orders, returns, churn.

A drive log has none of the three. Its authors are sensors with no intent, each sampling on its own schedule. Its identity is a scene, a moment and a set of tracked objects, none of which is a person you can phone. And the event that matters most, a serious crash, is so rare that a large fleet can drive for years without one, which tells you less than it seems to. Each absence alone is a caveat; together they change what counts as evidence.

Real world

NHTSA's summary of 2022 US traffic counts 42,514 people killed over 3,196,191 million vehicle miles, a rate of 1.33 deaths per 100 million miles. Read the other way round, that is one death for every 75.2 million miles driven by people. Any fleet that wants to say it beats that number is making a claim about events it will almost never see. Leader session 3 does the arithmetic.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back4 min▶
  • Frame. One capture from one sensor at one instant: an image, a lidar sweep, a radar return set.
  • Drive log. Every frame from every sensor on one vehicle for one drive, plus the vehicle's own state. The raw material for everything else.
  • Keyframe. A frame chosen for labelling. Labelling every frame is unaffordable, so teams label a sample and interpolate.
  • Scenario. A short piece of driving with a meaning: a cut-in, a pedestrian stepping out, a merge in rain.
  • Exposure. How much driving the evidence covers, usually in miles, and of which kind: highway, city, night, rain.
  • Operational design domain (ODD). The conditions a feature is designed to work in. Outside them it should not operate.
  • Disengagement. The automated mode switching off, because the system failed or a safety driver took over. A count of them measures the takeover policy as much as the car (leader session 4).
  • Constructed. Lanehart and every fleet number about it are invented so that the truth is knowable. The human baselines are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers the pipeline and the data team's seats

Five stages, each handing the next a guess

15 min live

The clearest way to see the car as a data problem is to draw what each stage passes on. Sensors produce frames. Perception turns frames into objects and lanes. Prediction turns objects into where each one will be in a few seconds. Planning picks a path through those futures. Control turns the path into steering and braking. Each stage is right most of the time and wrong in ways the next stage inherits. Under every stage sits a piece of data work.

LiveWhat each stage hands on, and how it goes wrong5 min▶

Sense. Cameras see colour and texture but not distance; lidar measures distance but is sparse far away; radar reaches furthest and measures speed but sees little shape. In a widely used public multi-sensor research dataset, the lidar range is typically 50 to 150 metres and radar reaches about 200. Each sensor samples at its own rate, so "the scene at 10:02:03.400" has to be assembled. Practitioner session 1 is that assembly.

Perceive. A detector turns frames into boxes with classes and confidences. It is a guess per frame, and its errors are not spread evenly: a pedestrian far away, half hidden, at night, is where it misses. Practitioner session 5 shows how one headline score hides that.

Predict and plan. Prediction gives each tracked object a few possible futures; planning chooses a path that is safe against them. A wrong guess upstream becomes a confident decision downstream. That is why the data team cannot evaluate one stage in isolation and call the car evaluated.

LiveThe five seats, as five questions4 min▶
  • Drive logs: can we reassemble any moment from every sensor, on one clock? If not, nothing downstream can be trusted.
  • Labels: which frames did we pay to label, and were the rare ones among them? A label is bought by the frame; leader session 2 and practitioner session 2 price it.
  • Evaluation slices: is a score reported only as one number, or by distance band and scenario, where the failures live?
  • Simulation: for the situations the fleet almost never meets, how are they tested, and how is the result weighted back to real-world rates?
  • Safety claim: a rate needs a numerator and a denominator. Which events, over how many miles, of which kind?
Self-studyA frame is not a row4 min▶

The public dataset's paper describes six cameras at 12 Hz, one spinning lidar at 20 Hz and five radars at 13 Hz. A camera's exposure is triggered when the lidar sweeps across the centre of that camera's view, and the paper notes that, because 12 exposures are spread across 20 lidar scans, not every lidar scan has a matching camera frame. Labels are applied to keyframes sampled at 2 Hz.

So the smallest unit the data team works with is not a row but a synchronised bundle, most of which was never labelled. Warehouse habits about primary keys and one row per event have to be rebuilt around time.

Part 2 · covers the SAE levels, read from the chart

A level belongs to a feature, not to a car

10 min live

The SAE J3016 levels are the most quoted vocabulary in the industry and the most misread. The chart SAE publishes free with the 2021 revision says plainly who is driving. At Levels 0 to 2 "you are driving whenever these driver support features are engaged", even with your feet off the pedals. At Levels 3 to 5 "you are not driving" while the feature is engaged. The line that matters most for data work sits at Level 3.

LiveThree misreadings to stop on sight4 min▶

"This is a Level 4 car." The chart classifies features. A vehicle can carry a Level 2 feature on the highway and nothing else in town. Ask which feature, engaged where.

"Level 2 is nearly self-driving." The chart says that at Level 2 you must constantly supervise and steer, brake or accelerate as needed. The person is the safety system. Data from Level 2 driving is data about a human and a machine together.

"Level 4 means it drives anywhere." The chart says Level 4 features drive "under limited conditions and will not operate unless all required conditions are met". Those conditions are the operational design domain. Every rate a Level 4 fleet reports is a rate inside its domain, which is why leader session 4 asks about exposure before anything else.

LiveWho reports a crash, and what the count leaves out3 min▶

NHTSA's Standing General Order, first issued in 2021 and amended in 2021, 2023 and 2025, requires named manufacturers and operators to report certain crashes when an automated driving system (Levels 3 to 5) or a Level 2 system was in use within 30 seconds of the crash. The page is candid about what that data is not: the counts "have not" been "normalized or adjusted by any measure of exposure, including operational design domains or vehicle miles traveled".

A count with no miles behind it cannot be compared with anything. That sentence, from the regulator itself, is the first thing a leader should know about public crash numbers. Leader session 4 builds on it.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

When features at Level 4 will be broadly available, in how many places, and whether Level 5 is reachable at all, are open questions. Forecasts have been made and missed in both directions, and the people closest to the work disagree. No page here gives a date.

What is not contested is the arithmetic of proof. Kalra and Paddock (2016), in RAND's "Driving to Safety", show that driving alone would take hundreds of millions of miles, and for some questions billions, to demonstrate safety against human rates, and conclude that test driving alone cannot provide sufficient evidence. That conclusion is where the long tail, simulation and importance sampling come in.

Part 3 · build-along

Five questions to put to any driving-data claim

17 min hands on

No code. Take one claim you have seen or will be asked to sign off, about a fleet, a feature or a dataset, and run it through five questions in order. The figure is why the order matters: the events that decide safety are so rare that intuition about "a lot of miles" fails.

Write the claim down in one sentence

Exactly as it was made: "our feature is safer than human drivers", "the new detector is 98 percent accurate", "the fleet drove 150 million miles without a fatality". Underline the number and the comparison word.

Ask which stage it is about

Perception, prediction, planning, or the whole car? A perception score says nothing about the car. Put the claim on the pipeline in the first figure.

Ask which feature, at which level, inside which domain

Name the feature, its level from the chart, and the conditions it was operating in. If the claim is about a Level 2 feature, write "a person was supervising" next to it.

Ask for the numerator and the denominator

Which events were counted (all crashes, reported crashes, injuries, deaths) and over how many miles of which kind. If there is no denominator, write "not comparable" and stop there; the regulator's own crash page says exactly that about raw counts.

Ask what was never seen

Which rare situations did the evidence not include, and how were they tested instead? A blank here is the most important answer on the page. Leader session 2 is about it.

Commit to a guess about Lanehart

Lanehart's pilot reports zero fatal crashes in 150 million constructed miles. Write down whether you think that proves it beats the human rate of 1.33 per 100 million. Leader session 3 checks the guess.

The habit to keep. When someone shows you a driving-data number, do not ask whether it is good. Ask which stage, which feature in which domain, what was counted over how many miles, and what was never seen. A claim with no denominator is not a small claim; it is no claim.

Check yourself

Three questions

Lanehart's pilot (constructed) reports zero fatal crashes over 150 million miles. US drivers had 1.33 deaths per 100 million miles in 2022. What is the right reading?

Zero events still carry information, which is why C is wrong: the bound is about 3 divided by the miles. But 150 million miles is not enough for that bound to drop below the human rate. Leader session 3 does the arithmetic.

A feature is described as Level 3 and is engaged. What does the SAE chart say the person in the driver's seat must do?

A describes Levels 0 to 2. B describes Levels 4 and 5. Level 3 is the only level with a planned handover, which is why its logs must record who was in control and when the request came.

A public dashboard lists crash reports by company under the federal crash-reporting order. What can a leader conclude from one company having more reports than another?

A rate needs a denominator. NHTSA's own page says reporting entities need not report vehicles operated or miles traveled. Both B and C read a numerator as if it were a rate.

Homework

Twenty minutes, on one claim and on your guess about Lanehart.
