Curating for the tail

Session 3 mined the fleet for rare events. Now the mined frames have to be put somewhere, and they go into two sets with opposite rules. The training set may be rebalanced however helps the model learn. The evaluation set has to keep every tail slice and weight each one by how often the road actually serves it, or the safety claim turns green for the wrong reason. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies the slices; its numbers come from the course engine's seeded canon run, and the build-along table is constructed.

Part 0

Lanehart's evaluation set got tidier, and the fleet got "safer"

Lanehart is a constructed robotaxi pilot. A well-meaning clean-up removed four small scenario slices from its evaluation set: a pedestrian stepping out from behind a parked van, a wrong-way vehicle on a divided road, debris in the lane at highway speed, an emergency vehicle crossing a junction. They were small, noisy and hard to label. Nothing about the fleet changed. Its crash-rate claim moved from worse than the human baseline to proven better. This session is about why that happens and the one habit that stops it.

Live = worked through in the 45 minutes Self-study = the full depth, after class 🔍 Every diagram zooms

★ What you walk out with tonight. The split between a training mix, which you may rebalance freely, and evaluation slices, which must be weighted by exposure. The tail as encounters times failure probability: Lanehart's four constructed classes give 12 + 6 + 10 + 18 = 46 crashes per 100M miles. The break button read off the bench: with every slice kept, 192.24 per 100M (upper bound 215.71), worse than the 185.55 human baseline; with the tail slices dropped, 147.74 (upper bound 169.04), falsely proven. And a slice table in pandas that shows three weightings giving 84, 0 and 46, only one of which is a rate.

LiveWhere the curated frames come from4 min▶

Logged miles under-serve the tail. A random sample of driving is mostly ordinary driving. In the bench's canon run, 2,000 randomly drawn encounters with the occluded pedestrian produced 1 failure, while the same budget with the hard cases deliberately over-drawn produced 743 failures to learn from. Rare events have to be sought out.

So they are mined and curated. Session 3's scenario queries pull candidate frames for each rare class, and somebody decides how many go where. That decision is the subject tonight, because the same frames feed two sets that need opposite treatment.

Real world

RAND's "Driving to Safety" (Kalra and Paddock, 2016) concludes that developers cannot simply drive their way to safety: test driving alone cannot provide enough evidence about fatalities and injuries, so other methods such as simulation and scenario testing are needed. Scenario testing is only as honest as the scenario set, which is a curated dataset like any other.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Slice. The frames or encounters of one scenario class, evaluated as a group.
  • Encounter rate. How often the road serves that scenario, per 100M miles. The exposure.
  • Failure probability. The chance the car fails on one encounter, measured inside the slice.
  • Training mix. The proportions the model learns from. A design choice.
  • Exposure weighting. Turning slice results into a rate by multiplying each by its encounter rate.
  • Break button. The bench control that drops the four tail slices from evaluation, so their rate becomes 0.
  • Constructed. Lanehart's classes, rates and probabilities are generated by the seeded engine, not recorded.
Part 1 · covers training balance vs evaluation slices

One curated pile, two sets, two rules

15 min live

The bench curates 2,000 encounters for each of Lanehart's four rare classes. Equal slices are good for measuring each failure probability. They say nothing about how often the road serves each class: the occluded pedestrian turns up 4,000 times per 100M miles and the wrong-way vehicle 300 times. The slice's share of the set and its share of the claim are different numbers.

LiveTraining: rebalance freely5 min▶

A model that sees the occluded pedestrian a handful of times in a million ordinary frames will barely learn it. So the training mix is curated: rare classes are over-drawn, repeated or up-weighted, and common, easy frames are thinned. None of that is cheating. The training set's job is to teach, and its proportions are a design lever you are free to pull.

What you owe is a record. Write down the mix you chose and why, because the next person to read a training-set summary will otherwise assume the proportions describe the road.

LiveEvaluation: keep every slice, weight by exposure5 min▶

The evaluation set has a different job: to estimate a rate on the road. That splits into two steps. Inside each slice, measure the failure probability; equal or deliberately skewed slices are fine here, provided the skew is undone. Then multiply each slice's result by how often the road serves it, and add. For Lanehart that gives 12 + 6 + 10 + 18 = 46 crashes per 100M miles of tail on top of the ordinary driving.

Two failure modes, both quiet. Weight the slices by their share of the curated set and the rare, dangerous wrong-way slice is counted as if it were as common as the occluded pedestrian (the build-along shows this gives 84, not 46). Drop a slice and its contribution becomes exactly 0, which is Part 2.

Real world

Zhao et al. (2017, IEEE T-ITS) evaluated lane-change scenarios built from real cut-in data, skewed the statistics toward riskier behaviour so failures show up, then reweighted the estimates back to real-world rates. The authors report acceleration of 2,000 to 20,000 times. Skew to find the failures, reweight to state the rate: the same two steps, written down in a paper.

Self-studyWhere label quality fits3 min▶

Tail slices are small and their labels are often disputed, which is exactly why they get cleaned away. Label noise and how to clean it is owned by learn data-centric AI. The rule here is narrower: a noisy tail slice is relabelled or flagged, never removed from evaluation, because removing it sets its rate to 0.

Part 2 · covers dropping tail slices from evaluation

The break button: the claim turns green and nothing got safer

10 min live

The bench in session 10 has a break button that drops the four tail slices from evaluation. Run on the canon settings (150M constructed miles, 95% bounds), the exposure-matched crash rate is 147.74 per 100M and the importance-sampled tail adds 44.50, for 192.24 with an upper bound of 215.71: worse than the human baseline of 185.55. Press the button and the claim is 147.74 with an upper bound of 169.04, below the baseline: "proven". Same fleet, same miles.

LiveWhy a missing slice reads as zero4 min▶

An evaluation rate is a sum over slices. A slice that is not in the set contributes nothing to the sum, and nothing is the same number as "never fails". No error is raised, no row is null, the total simply gets smaller. The tail slices are the ones most likely to be cut (small, expensive to label, argued over), and they are also where the risk sits: on the canon run, 44.50 of the 192.24.

Notice what the bound does not do. The 95% upper bound on 147.74 is honest about sampling noise in the slices that remain. It cannot see a slice that is gone. Statistical rigour on a curated set inherits every curation decision.

LiveThe same shape in public reporting3 min▶

A slice without its encounter rate is a numerator without a denominator, and a missing slice is a numerator nobody wrote down. Both are reading problems, not arithmetic problems.

Real world

NHTSA's Standing General Order page warns that the crash counts it publishes "have not ... been normalized or adjusted by any measure of exposure, including operational design domains or vehicle miles traveled." A count with no exposure cannot be turned into a rate; a slice with no encounter rate cannot be turned into its share of a claim.

Self-studyMaking the tail slice cheaper to estimate3 min▶

On the canon run, a random 2,000 tries per class estimates the tail at 34.95 per 100M against a true 46; the occluded pedestrian slice saw 1 failure and read as 2.00 against a true 12. Importance sampling over-draws the hard cases and reweights them, and estimates 44.50. That is practitioner session 8. The curation rule is the same either way: the slice stays in, and its weight is known.

Part 3 · build-along

Build a slice table and weight it three ways

17 min hands on

Build Lanehart's four tail slices as a table in pandas, with the curated tries and the expected failures at the canon failure probabilities (constructed). Then weight it three ways and see which one is a rate. The expected counts make the arithmetic exact; the optional sampled version will give numbers that differ from run to run.

Build the slice table

Four tail classes, the road's encounter rate for each, 2,000 curated tries per class, and the failures you would expect at Lanehart's constructed failure probabilities (0.003, 0.02, 0.004, 0.015).

pythonimport pandas as pd slices = pd.DataFrame({ "cls": ["occluded pedestrian", "wrong-way vehicle", "debris in lane", "emergency vehicle"], "enc_per_100M": [4000, 300, 2500, 1200], "tried": [2000, 2000, 2000, 2000], "failed": [6, 40, 8, 30], }) slices["p_fail"] = slices.failed / slices.tried slices

Weight it the tempting way: by the curated share

Pool the failures over the tries (84 of 8,000, 1.05%) and apply that to all 8,000 encounters per 100M. You get 84. It treats every slice as equally common, so the wrong-way slice, rare on the road but often failed, counts as if it were as common as the occluded pedestrian.

pythonpooled = slices.failed.sum() / slices.tried.sum() pooled, pooled * slices.enc_per_100M.sum()

Weight it by exposure

Multiply each slice's failure probability by its encounter rate and add: 12 + 6 + 10 + 18 = 46 per 100M miles. This is the only one of the three that is a rate on the road.

pythonslices["per_100M"] = slices.enc_per_100M * slices.p_fail slices[["cls", "per_100M"]], slices.per_100M.sum()

Press the break button by hand

Filter the four classes out, as a clean-up script would, and sum again. The answer is 0.0, with no warning. Then add each answer to the exposure-matched 147.74 from the canon run: with the true 46 the total is 193.74, above the human 185.55; with 0 it is 147.74, below it.

pythonTAIL = ["occluded pedestrian", "wrong-way vehicle", "debris in lane", "emergency vehicle"] kept = slices[~slices.cls.isin(TAIL)] kept.per_100M.sum(), 147.74 + slices.per_100M.sum(), 147.74 + kept.per_100M.sum()

Add a guard that refuses to run without the tail

Make the evaluation job fail loudly when a required slice is missing or has no encounter rate. A guard turns a silent 0 into an error somebody has to answer.

pythondef check_slices(df, required): missing = set(required) - set(df.cls) assert not missing, f"tail slices missing from evaluation: {sorted(missing)}" assert df.enc_per_100M.notna().all(), "a slice has no encounter rate" check_slices(slices, TAIL) check_slices(kept, TAIL)

Optional: sample the failures instead

Replace the expected counts with a seeded binomial draw and rerun the three weightings. Your numbers will differ from 84, 0 and 46 and from run to run; the ordering of the three methods is the point, not the decimals.

pythonimport numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(4) slices["failed"] = rng.binomial(slices.tried, [0.003, 0.02, 0.004, 0.015])

The habit to keep. Keep two manifests. The training manifest records the mix you chose. The evaluation manifest lists every required slice with its encounter rate and its source, and the evaluation job refuses to run if any slice is missing. Rebalance the first as you like; never let the second shrink without a written reason.

Check yourself

Three questions

The training set over-draws the four rare classes so the model sees enough of them. Is that a problem?

Rebalancing to help learning is legitimate. The mistake is carrying the curated proportions into the claim, which on the build-along table turns 46 into 84.

On the canon run, dropping the four tail slices moves Lanehart from 192.24 (upper 215.71) to 147.74 (upper 169.04) against a human 185.55. What changed?

The fleet, its miles and its true tail of 46 per 100M are untouched. The bound is honest about the slices that remain and blind to the ones removed.

The wrong-way slice has the highest failure probability (0.02) but contributes only 6 per 100M. Why?

300 x 0.02 = 6. The occluded pedestrian fails far less often per encounter (0.003) but is met 4,000 times, so it contributes 12.

Before session 5

Homework

Thirty minutes, in your own notebook.

Add a fifth slice and decide its weight

Invent a rare class of your own and give it an encounter rate and a failure probability, labelled constructed. Before you add it, write down where a real encounter rate would come from (logged miles, a scenario query from session 3, a public statistic). Recompute the tail; your numbers will differ.

Find one evaluation set that only ever shrinks

In any system you work with, find a test or evaluation set whose rows have been removed over time for being noisy or hard. List what was removed and ask what each removal did to the headline number.

Write your evaluation manifest

One line per required slice: name, encounter rate, its source, its minimum size. Wire the guard from the build-along to it.
