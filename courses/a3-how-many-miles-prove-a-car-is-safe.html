How many miles prove a car is safe?

A fleet drives a long way without a single death and someone puts "zero" on a slide. Zero is an observation, not a rate, and the honest question is how high the true rate could still be. Tonight is RAND's arithmetic of proof, the bound that slides down as miles pile up, and a calculator you can point at any claim. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, is the worked case; every Lanehart number is generated, not observed. The human rates are NHTSA's and RAND's, read at source.

Part 0

Lanehart's slide: zero fatal crashes in 150 million miles

At the end of leader session 1 you committed to a guess. Lanehart, the constructed pilot fleet, reports zero fatal crashes over 150 million constructed miles, and the safety lead wants to write "safer than human drivers" on the board pack. People on US roads in 2022 died at 1.33 per 100 million miles. Zero is smaller than 1.33. Tonight you find out why that comparison is not the one that matters, and what number would be.

★ What you walk out with tonight. RAND's three questions and what each one costs in miles: 275 million to show a fatality rate no worse than humans with zero deaths, about 8.8 billion to measure the rate to within 20%, about 11 billion to show it is 20% better. The rule of three, which turns "nothing happened" into an upper bound you can say out loud. Why Lanehart's 150 million clean miles give a 95% bound of 2.00 deaths per 100 million, still above 1.33, and why the line is crossed only at 225.2 million. And a calculator that prices any zero-event claim in seconds.

LiveThe two numbers on the slide are not the same kind of thing3 min▶

1.33 is a rate measured over an enormous amount of driving: 42,514 deaths across 3,196,191 million vehicle miles in 2022. Zero is a count from a much smaller pile of miles. If the true Lanehart rate were exactly the human rate, the fleet would expect only a couple of deaths in 150 million miles, and seeing none would be unremarkable. So "zero" cannot tell the two possibilities apart yet.

The fair question is: given zero deaths in these miles, what is the highest rate we cannot rule out? That number, an upper confidence bound, is what you compare with 1.33. If the bound is below the human line, the claim is proven at that confidence. If only the observation is below it, the claim is not proven. Those three words, "proven", "not proven", "worse", are the whole vocabulary of tonight.

Real world

NHTSA's 2022 summary (DOT HS 813 643) puts the US fatality rate at 1.33 per 100 million vehicle miles; over the ten years 2013 to 2022 it ranged from 1.08 (2014) to 1.38 (2021). The human line a fleet must beat is itself a moving target, which matters later tonight.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Exposure. The miles the evidence covers. Every rate here is per 100 million vehicle miles.
  • Point estimate. Events divided by miles. With zero events it is zero, which is why it misleads.
  • Upper confidence bound. The highest true rate still consistent with what was seen, at a stated confidence such as 95%.
  • Zero-event bound. The upper bound when nothing happened. At 95% it is about 3 events divided by the miles: the rule of three.
  • Proven, not proven, worse. Bound below the human rate; only the point below it; the point itself at or above it.
  • Constructed. Lanehart and its miles are invented so the truth is knowable. The human baselines are real and cited.
Part 1 · covers RAND's three questions and Table 1

Three questions, three very different mileages

15 min live

Kalra and Paddock (2016), in RAND's "Driving to Safety", asked how many miles of driving it would take to demonstrate the reliability of an automated vehicle, using the 2013 human rate of 1.09 deaths per 100 million miles. The answer depends entirely on which question you ask. "Is it no worse than people?", "how bad is it, exactly?" and "is it clearly better?" sound alike in a board meeting and differ by a factor of forty in miles.

LiveQuestion one: no worse than people, with zero deaths5 min▶

Suppose the fleet never kills anyone. How far must it drive before "never" is evidence? RAND solves it as a reliability demonstration: the miles n at which, if the true rate were the human one, seeing zero deaths would happen less than 5% of the time. Their equation is n = ln(1 - C) / ln(R), with C the confidence and R the chance of surviving one mile. At 95% and 1.09 deaths per 100 million, it gives 275 million failure-free miles.

To make that tangible, RAND imagines 100 vehicles driving 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, at 25 mph. That fleet needs about 12.5 years to cover 275 million miles. This is the cheapest of the three questions, and it only shows "no worse than", never "better".

Real world

Kalra and Paddock (2016), Driving to Safety, RAND RR-1478, doi:10.7249/RR1478, read at source. Their conclusion: developers "cannot simply drive" their way to safety; test driving alone cannot provide sufficient evidence for fatalities and injuries, and other methods such as simulation, scenario testing and staged deployment are needed. Leader session 2 is where those other methods start.

LiveQuestions two and three: how precise, and how much better5 min▶

Precision. To estimate the fatality rate to within 20% at 95% confidence, you need to observe about 96 deaths. At a rate near 1.09 that is about 8.8 billion miles (RAND prints 8,811,009,174), about 400 years for the same 100-vehicle fleet. A precise rate needs events, and events are deaths.

Superiority. To show with 95% confidence and 80% power that the fleet's rate is 20% better than 1.09 takes 11 billion miles. The report disagrees with itself on the time: its text says 518 years and Table 1 says 500, so quote it as "about 500 years". To show 5% better takes 215 billion miles, nearly 10,000 years.

The pattern to remember: the smaller the improvement you want to prove, the more miles you need, and it grows fast. The same logic governs any A/B test; learn experimentation, power and sample size owns the general method.

Self-studyInjuries and crashes are cheaper to prove3 min▶

RAND's Table 1 repeats the three questions for more common events, using 2013 human benchmarks of 77 reported injuries and 190 reported crashes per 100 million miles. For injuries the three answers are 3.9 million, 125 million and 161 million miles. For crashes they are 1.6 million, 51 million and 65 million.

Common events are cheap to measure, which is why fleets report crash and injury rates long before they can say anything about deaths. The catch is comparability: police-reported human crashes undercount all crashes, and a fleet with full telemetry records more of its minor ones. Whether those two counts can be compared like for like is contested, and serious people disagree. Leader session 4 takes it apart.

Self-studyWhy RAND's numbers say 20132 min▶

The report was written with 2013 data: 32,719 deaths, 1.09 per 100 million miles. NHTSA's current table gives 32,893 for 2013 and a rate of 1.10, a later revision. The difference does not change the lesson, but it is a good habit to notice which year's baseline a claim is measured against, because the next Part shows how much the answer moves when the baseline does.

Part 2 · covers the rule of three, the exact bound and the 2022 baseline

Zero is not a rate: the bound that slides with miles

10 min live

When nothing has happened, the upper bound at 95% confidence is -ln(0.05) = 2.996 events, spread over the miles driven. That is the rule of three: about 3 divided by the exposure. As the miles grow, the same 2.996 is spread thinner and the bound slides down. The claim is proven on the day the bound crosses the human line, and not before.

LiveThe rule of three, and where it comes from4 min▶

If deaths arrive at random at some rate over miles, the chance of seeing none in a stretch where you would expect m of them is e to the power -m. Set that chance to 5% and solve: m = -ln(0.05) = 2.996. So with zero events, any true expectation above about 3 events would have made "zero" a less than 5% surprise. Divide 2.996 by the miles and you have the 95% upper bound on the rate.

For Lanehart: 2.996 over 150 million miles is 2.00 per 100 million. That is above 1.33, so the verdict is not proven. Run it the other way: 2.996 divided by 1.33 per 100 million is 225.2 million miles. Lanehart has driven two thirds of the way to proof.

Real world

The shortcut is old and comes from medicine. Hanley and Lippman-Hand (1983), "If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right? Interpreting zero numerators", JAMA 249(13):1743-1745, is the reported source for the rule that with 0 events in n trials the 95% upper limit is about 3/n. The result itself is derivable, as above.

LiveWhy 275 million became 225.2 million3 min▶

Same maths, same 95%, different target. RAND compared against 1.09 deaths per 100 million (2013). The 2022 rate is 1.33, so the line sits higher and the falling bound reaches it sooner: 225.2 million instead of 275 million miles. Nothing about any car improved. The human rate got worse.

Two lessons for a leader. First, always ask which year's human rate a claim is measured against; across 2013 to 2022 it ranged from 1.08 to 1.38. Second, confidence is a choice with a price: with zero deaths against 1.33, 90% needs 173.1 million miles and 99% needs 346.3 million. Ask which level was chosen, and who chose it.

Self-studyWhen events do happen: the exact bound4 min▶

The rule of three is the k = 0 case of a general result. For k observed events at confidence C, the exact one-sided upper bound on the expected count (the Garwood form) is the chi-square quantile at C with 2k + 2 degrees of freedom, divided by 2; equivalently the gamma quantile with shape k + 1. For k = 0 it is exactly -ln(1 - C). RAND notes that with fewer than 30 events an exact interval should be used, which is why the calculator above uses the exact form rather than a normal approximation.

Try the calculator with one or two deaths at the same miles: the bound jumps, because each event is a large share of a small count. Rare-event rates are fragile in both directions. Practitioner session 7 builds the bound by hand; the general idea of a confidence interval lives in learn statistics, confidence intervals.

Self-studyAll crashes clear the bar sooner, and that is not the end3 min▶

Deaths are so rare that the fatal bound is the hardest to clear. All crashes are common: the human baseline, derived from NHTSA 2022 police-reported crashes, is 185.55 per 100 million miles. On its own mix of roads, Lanehart's constructed fleet logs 180 crashes in 150 million miles, a rate of 120.00 with an upper bound of 135.79, comfortably "proven".

Comfortably, but on the wrong miles. Lanehart drives mostly highway, people drive more city, night and rain. Leader session 4 re-weights the same crashes to the human mix of roads and shows how the margin narrows, and practitioner session 10 adds the rare scenarios that turn the verdict around.

Part 3 · build-along

Price a zero-event claim on one sheet

17 min hands on

Pen, paper and the calculator above. You will turn "zero deaths in X miles" into a verdict and a distance still to go, then see how much the answer depends on two choices nobody puts on the slide: the confidence level and the human baseline.

Write the claim as two numbers

Events and miles: "0 deaths, 150 million miles" for Lanehart, or any claim you have seen. If the claim has no miles, stop and write "not comparable"; there is nothing to bound.

Bound it by hand with the rule of three

Divide 2.996 by the miles and multiply by 100 million. For Lanehart that gives 2.00. Then put the same numbers into the calculator and check it agrees.

Give the verdict in one word

Bound below the human rate: proven. Only the observed rate below it: not proven. Observed rate at or above it: worse. Lanehart against 1.33 is not proven.

Work out the distance still to go

Miles needed with zero events are 2.996 divided by the human rate per mile: 225.2 million against 1.33. Subtract what has been driven. Then ask how long the fleet takes to drive the gap at its current pace; write your answer down, your numbers will differ.

Move the two hidden choices

In the calculator, switch confidence to 90% and 99%, then switch the baseline to RAND's 1.09. Note the four finish lines: 173.1, 225.2, 346.3 and about 275 million. A claim that does not state both choices has not told you its finish line.

Add one death and watch

Set deaths to 1 at the same miles. Write the new bound next to the old one. That jump is why one event can reverse a confident story, and why practitioner session 7 never reports a rare rate without its bound.

The habit to keep. Never compare a count of zero with a rate. Ask for the upper bound at a stated confidence, against a stated year's human rate, and for the miles still needed if it is not yet proven. And remember RAND's point: for deaths, driving alone takes hundreds of millions of miles even to say "no worse", so the rest of the evidence has to come from somewhere else.

Check yourself

Three questions

RAND's report says 275 million failure-free miles; this page says 225.2 million for the same 95% zero-death question. Why the difference?

The calculation is the same, which rules out A, and it says nothing about any vehicle, which rules out B. Only the target moved. Always ask which year's human rate a claim uses.

Lanehart (constructed) reports zero fatal crashes in 150 million miles. What is the honest verdict against 1.33 per 100 million at 95%?

B compares a count with a rate. C ignores that zero events still bound the rate, at about 3 divided by the miles. The verdict is "not proven", with a known distance still to go.

A board member asks for 99% confidence instead of 95%. With zero deaths, against 1.33, how many miles does the fleet need?

The zero-event bound is -ln(1 - C) divided by the miles. At 99% the numerator grows from 2.996 to -ln(0.01), so more miles are needed. 173.1 million is the 90% answer.
