Labels cost by the frame

In a warehouse the label usually arrives with the row: the order was refunded or it was not. In a drive log, ground truth is bought. Somebody draws a box around every car, cyclist and cone on a chosen frame, and the bill grows with every frame you choose. This session prices that bill as a product of four numbers, shows which one is the lever, and treats checking the labels as what it is, a sample with a bound. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies the budget; every cost is in constructed units and every count is computed in your browser.

Part 0

Lanehart's first labelling quote

Lanehart is a constructed robotaxi pilot. Its data lead was asked a warehouse-shaped question: "how much to label last month's driving?" She asked back four questions, and the answer fell out of them. How many hours. How many frames per second get labelled. How many objects sit on a typical frame. What one box costs, drawn or checked. Then a fifth question that nobody had budgeted for: how will we know the boxes are right?

LiveWhy labels are bought, not logged3 min▶

A sensor records what it saw. It does not record what the things were. "That cluster of lidar points is a cyclist, 1.7 m tall, heading north" is an annotation, and in the public dataset it is a 3D cuboid with position, size and yaw, one of 23 classes with 8 attributes, drawn on a keyframe.

So the unit of cost is a box on a frame. Everything a perception model later learns, and everything session 5 measures it against, rests on boxes somebody paid for. The question is never "do we label" but "which frames, how many, and how do we check them".

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Keyframe. A frame chosen for human labelling; all sensors at that instant.
  • Keyframe rate. Keyframes per second of driving; 2 Hz in the public dataset.
  • Box. One annotated object on one keyframe (a cuboid in 3D).
  • Interpolation. Filling labels between keyframes from the labels on either side.
  • Auto-labelling. A model proposes boxes; a person accepts, fixes or rejects them.
  • Audit. Re-checking a random sample of labelled frames to estimate the error rate.
  • Constructed. Lanehart's hours, object counts and cost units are inputs you choose, not records.
Part 1 · covers keyframes and annotation economics

The bill is a product of four numbers

15 min live

A camera at 12 Hz makes 12 frames a second. At 2 Hz keyframes, two of those get boxes and ten never do. Write the bill as a chain and every term is something you can look up or decide: hours driven, times 3,600 seconds, times the keyframe rate, times boxes per keyframe, times the cost of one box. Change any one and the bill moves in proportion. The keyframe rate is the one people forget is a choice.

LiveWhy 2 Hz is affordable, and what it gives up5 min▶

The public dataset labels keyframes at 2 Hz, and its authors report that 2 Hz annotations are robust to interpolation to 10 or 20 Hz. That finding is what makes sparse labelling a sound economic choice: an object that persists across keyframes can be filled in between them almost for free.

The cost of that choice lands in one place. Interpolation needs an object on both sides. Something that appears and is gone within half a second, a child stepping out from behind a van and back, may sit on no keyframe at all, and then no amount of interpolation labels it. A label budget set by the clock spends almost all of its money on ordinary frames, which is why sessions 3 and 4 go looking for the rare ones on purpose.

Real world

The public dataset's paper reports 1,000 scenes of about 20 seconds, six cameras at 12 Hz and 40,000 labelled keyframes against 1.4 million camera images. The arithmetic checks: 1,000 x 20 x 2 = 40,000 keyframes, and 1,000 x 20 x 12 x 6 = 1.44 million images. Each keyframe carries six camera images, so 240,000 of the 1.44 million images, about one in six, sit at a labelled instant. Derived from the paper's own numbers.

LiveWhich term to argue about first4 min▶
  • Hours driven. Scope. "All of last month" is rarely the question; "the hours that contain what we need" usually is, and that is a query (session 3).
  • Keyframe rate. The lever. Going from 2 Hz to 20 Hz is ten times the bill for the same hours. It should be set from what interpolation can and cannot recover, not from what the sensor produces.
  • Boxes per keyframe. Not under your control, and not constant: a dense junction carries many more objects than an empty highway, so the same hours cost different amounts in different places.
  • Cost per box. Depends on how the box is made. Drawing from scratch, fixing a proposal and accepting a proposal are three different prices. That is Part 2.

Part 2 · covers auto-labelling and label QA as sampling

Checking labels is a sample, so it has a bound

10 min live

Nobody re-checks every box; that would double the bill. So a team audits a random sample of labelled keyframes, counts the ones with errors, and reports an error rate. A count from a sample is an estimate with uncertainty, and when the count is zero the uncertainty is the whole story. It is the same maths as the miles question in session 7: audited frames are the exposure, errors are the events.

LiveAuto-labelling moves the cost, it does not remove it4 min▶

An auto-labeller is a model that proposes boxes on keyframes, often from many frames on either side, and a person reviews them. The bill changes shape: fewer boxes drawn, many boxes reviewed. Review is cheaper per box than drawing, and it is not free, which is why the calculator prices it as its own line.

The risk changes shape too. A proposal model is weakest exactly where it has seen least, which is the tail. And a reviewer's errors and a drawer's errors are different errors: a missed object that the model never proposed has nothing on screen to reject. So the audit has to be split by how a box was made, drawn by hand or proposed and accepted, and each group measured on its own.

LiveZero errors is a bound, not a zero4 min▶

Audit n frames at random and find k with errors. The point estimate is k / n. The one-sided upper bound at confidence C is the exact Poisson upper limit for k events divided by n; for k = 0 that limit is -ln(1 - C), which is 2.996 at 95%. So zero errors in 300 audited frames gives an upper bound of 2.996 / 300, about 1.0%. To show an error rate below 0.1% with zero errors you need about 2,996 audited frames. (Poisson is the small-rate approximation to the binomial; for rates this low the two agree closely.)

Real world

Hanley and Lippman-Hand's 1983 paper in JAMA, "If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right?", is reported as giving the rule of three: with zero events in n trials, the 95% upper limit is about 3 / n. The 3 is -ln(0.05) = 2.996 rounded. The same rule sits under RAND's 275 million failure-free miles in session 7; here it says how many frames a clean audit must cover.

Self-studyStratify the audit, and where cleaning lives4 min▶

A uniform audit of 300 keyframes, drawn from a pile that is mostly ordinary driving, may contain a handful of tail frames or none. Its bound is honest about the pile and silent about the tail. The fix is the one survey statisticians use: split the frames into strata (ordinary frames, each tail slice, auto-accepted boxes, hand-drawn boxes) and audit each with its own n. A clean result then reads "below about 1% in every stratum at 95%", which is a claim a reviewer can check.

What to do once errors are found, how labellers disagree, how to relabel and how noisy labels hurt training, is owned by learn data-centric AI. The side picked here is narrower: what labels cost per frame, and how much checking a claim about them needs.

Part 3 · build-along

Price a labelling job and audit it in pandas

17 min hands on

Build the calculator's bill from a scene table, then plant a known error rate in constructed labels and see what a random audit can and cannot tell you. Run it in a notebook or a scratch folder. The figure is the plan: one chain for the bill, one seeded draw for the audit.

Make a constructed scene table

Two hundred scenes of 20 seconds, like the public dataset's scene length, with a constructed object count per scene. Dense scenes carry more boxes; that is the point of drawing the counts from a range.

pythonimport numpy as np, pandas as pd rng = np.random.default_rng(7) scenes = pd.DataFrame({"scene_id": range(200), "seconds": 20.0, "objects": rng.integers(5, 60, size=200)}) scenes.describe()

Expand to keyframes and price the bill

One row per keyframe at 2 Hz. One unit is one hand-drawn box. Check the keyframe count by hand first: 200 x 20 x 2 = 8,000. Then change HZ to 20 and watch every total scale by ten.

pythonHZ = 2 kf = scenes.loc[scenes.index.repeat((scenes.seconds * HZ).astype(int))].copy() kf["t_s"] = kf.groupby("scene_id").cumcount() / HZ kf["boxes"] = kf["objects"] print(len(kf), "keyframes,", kf.boxes.sum(), "boxes,", kf.boxes.sum(), "units if all drawn")

Price an auto-labeller honestly

Say 80% of boxes are proposed and reviewed at 0.3 units each, both constructed. Print the saving as a share of the hand-drawn bill, then try a review cost of 0.5. The saving is never the 80% on the label.

pythonAUTO, REVIEW = 0.8, 0.3 b = kf.boxes.sum() bill = b * (1 - AUTO) * 1.0 + b * AUTO * REVIEW print(round(bill), "units;", round(1 - bill / b, 3), "saved vs all drawn")

Plant an error rate, then audit

Mark each keyframe wrong with a constructed probability of 0.5%. You know the truth; the audit does not. Draw 300 keyframes, count the wrong ones, and compute the exact Poisson upper bound divided by n.

pythonfrom scipy.stats import chi2 kf["wrong"] = rng.random(len(kf)) < 0.005 audit = kf.sample(300, random_state=7) k, n = int(audit.wrong.sum()), len(audit) upper = chi2.ppf(0.95, 2 * k + 2) / 2 / n print("true", kf.wrong.mean().round(4), "| audit k =", k, "| 95% upper", round(upper, 4))

Re-run the audit with ten seeds

Change random_state from 0 to 9. Count how many audits find k = 0 and still report an upper bound near 1%, and how often the bound sits above the true rate. That is what a 95% bound promises, and all it promises.

Write the audit rule down

One line per stratum: what is in it (hand-drawn, auto-accepted, each tail slice), its n, and the rate it must be shown below. That line belongs next to the label schema, beside the bill that paid for it.

The habit to keep. Before you quote a label budget, write it as hours x 3,600 x keyframe rate x boxes per keyframe x cost per box, and defend the rate out loud. Before you quote a label error rate, say how many frames were audited, how many were wrong, and the upper bound, per stratum. A clean audit is a bound, not a zero.

Check yourself

Three questions

Lanehart moves its keyframe rate from 2 Hz to 20 Hz, labelling every lidar scan, for the same hours of driving. What happens to the label bill?

Every extra frame still carries its boxes and each box still costs. The public dataset's authors report that 2 Hz labels interpolate well to 10 or 20 Hz, which is the argument for not paying the tenfold bill.

A team re-checks 300 randomly chosen keyframes and finds no errors. What can it claim?

Zero events gives an upper bound of -ln(0.05) / n, the rule of three. Showing below 0.1% would need about 2,996 clean frames. And a uniform sample from ordinary driving says little about tail slices it barely drew.

An auto-labeller now proposes 80% of Lanehart's boxes. Where should the audit go?

A proposal model is weakest where it has seen least, and a missed object it never proposed leaves a reviewer nothing to reject. Different ways of making a box make different errors, so each needs its own measurement.
