Why the warehouse playbook breaks here

A good data team arrives with habits that have never failed it: trust the row, join on the key, count the outcome, refresh the dashboard, fix it next run. On drive logs each habit meets a mechanism that quietly turns it against you. Tonight is five of them, drawn one at a time as a before and an after: the source, the identity, the ground truth, the feedback loop and the cost of being wrong. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies the fleet numbers; every one of them is generated, not observed. The human rates are NHTSA's.

Part 0

Lanehart's analyst opens her old playbook

Lanehart's data analyst, the one who came from a retail warehouse, has spent four sessions learning the vocabulary. This week she is asked to "port the safety dashboard to the standard stack": the same star schema, the same nightly refresh, the same KPI cards she built for sales. Lanehart is constructed, so we can watch what happens to each of her habits with the truth known. None of them is stupid. Each one was right for a row someone meant, and each one has a specific mechanism that makes it wrong for a frame a sensor took.

Five before-and-after drawings you can redraw on a whiteboard: a row against a moment assembled from sensors on their own clocks; a customer id against a scene, a track and a context; a label that arrives with the sale against a label bought by the frame and a death that arrives once in 75.2 million miles; a dashboard that reads the world against a fleet whose own takeover policy decides what gets logged; and a refund against a safety case with a missing leaf. LiveFive habits, five mechanisms3 min▶
  • "Trust the row." Breaks on the source: no one meant a lidar sweep; it is a measurement on its own clock.
  • "Join on the key." Breaks on the identity: the keys that matter are inferred by the pipeline and can split or swap.
  • "Count the outcome." Breaks on the ground truth: labels are bought, and the outcome that matters almost never happens.
  • "Refresh the dashboard." Breaks on the feedback loop: the fleet's policy changes which miles become data.
  • "Fix it next run." Breaks on the cost of being wrong: a missed case is not refunded, it is argued about in a safety case.

None of these is a slogan to repeat. Each is a drawing of what physically happens to the data, and a question to ask that the warehouse never needed.

Part 1 · covers the source and the identity

No one meant this row, and no one owns this key

13 min live

In the warehouse, a row has an author and a moment: a till recorded a sale at 09:14:07 because a person bought something. A drive log has no author. A lidar spins and stamps each sweep when the turn completes; each camera fires when the sweep passes its view; radars sample on a third schedule. "The scene at 10:02:03.400" does not exist until the pipeline assembles it, and the assembly is a choice.

LiveThe source: a measurement on its own clock4 min▶

A warehouse analyst's first instinct is to trust that a row is what it says. That instinct rests on intent: someone placed the order. A sensor has no intent. It reports what it measured at the instant its own schedule fired, and every sensor keeps a different schedule.

So the analyst's second instinct, "join on the nearest timestamp", inherits a bug from the first. Each timestamp means something different. A lidar stamp marks the end of a turn; a camera stamp marks an exposure triggered partway through one. The nearest stamp is often the wrong sweep. On Lanehart's constructed one-second sync toy, nearest-timestamp matching put 28 of 72 camera frames on the right scan and 44 on the wrong one; a forward join that respects what the stamps mean put 72 of 72 right. Practitioner session 1 builds that join by hand.

Real world

The paper for a public multi-sensor research dataset describes six cameras at 12 Hz, one spinning lidar at 20 Hz and five radars at 13 Hz. A camera's exposure is triggered when the lidar sweeps across the centre of that camera's view; the image timestamp is the trigger time and the lidar timestamp is when the full rotation completes. The authors add that the 12 exposures are spread across the 20 lidar scans, so not every scan has a matching camera frame. The design is careful, and it still means a moment is something you assemble.

LiveThe identity: keys the pipeline made up4 min▶

A customer id is assigned once and means one person for years. You can join a refund to an order to a support ticket and the join never guesses. The units of a drive log are different in kind. A scene is a slice of driving someone chose to cut, about 20 seconds in the public dataset. An actor track is an id a tracker gives an object while it can follow it. A context (urban, night, rain) is a tag attached afterwards, by a rule or a model.

Each is an inference, and inferences fail in patterned ways. A child who walks behind a parked van and out again can come back as a new track. Count "pedestrians per scene" and the number moves when the tracker improves, with no change on the road. Count "rain miles" and the number moves when the weather tagger changes. The warehouse question "is the key unique?" becomes "who made this key, how, and what happens to it when they change their mind?"

Self-studyWhy the occluded case is exactly the one that matters4 min▶

The track break in the figure is not a cosmetic nuisance. Lanehart's constructed rare classes include "a pedestrian steps out from behind a parked van": 4,000 encounters per 100 million miles, each failing with probability 0.003, for 12 failures per 100 million. The moment the identity layer is weakest is the moment the safety claim most depends on.

In the constructed canon, plain Monte Carlo sampling of that class at a budget of 2,000 encounters saw 1 failure; importance sampling saw 743 at the same budget. Leader session 2 and practitioner session 8 explain why. For tonight, hold the shape: identity errors and rare events live in the same frames.

Part 2 · covers the ground truth

A label you buy, and an outcome you wait for

6 min live

The warehouse is labelled by the business itself. Whether an order was returned arrives as another row, free, within weeks, for every order. Driving data has two ground truths and neither works that way. What was in the frame is bought from annotators, keyframe by keyframe. Whether the car is safe is decided by events so rare that they arrive on the road's schedule, not yours.

LiveTwo ground truths, neither of them free4 min▶

What was in the frame. In the public dataset, keyframes are annotated at 2 Hz with 3D boxes, classes and attributes, and the authors found 2 Hz labels robust to interpolation up to 10 to 20 Hz. Against a 20 Hz lidar, that is about one sweep in ten carrying a label someone paid for. The warehouse question "what share of rows are labelled?" has the answer "all of them" there; here the answer is a budget decision, and the frames you most need are the hardest to find. Practitioner session 2 prices it.

Whether the car is safe. NHTSA's 2022 summary gives 1.33 deaths per 100 million vehicle miles: one per 75.2 million. Police-reported crashes come out at 185.55 per 100 million (derived), about one per 538,938 miles. Lanehart's 150 million constructed miles with no deaths still bound its fatal rate at 2.00 per 100 million, above the human 1.33. The outcome exists; you cannot accelerate its arrival by driving harder.

Real world

Kalra and Paddock (2016), in RAND's "Driving to Safety", calculate that showing a fatality rate at or below the 2013 human rate of 1.09 per 100 million, at 95% confidence, takes 275 million failure-free miles; estimating the rate to within 20% takes about 8.8 billion miles. Their conclusion is that developers cannot simply drive their way to the answer: test driving alone cannot provide sufficient evidence for fatalities and injuries.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: is the crash count comparable?3 min▶

Even the plentiful outcome, crashes, has a ground-truth problem a warehouse never meets. The human baseline counts police-reported crashes, which undercount all crashes; RAND keeps separate "reported" and "estimated total" rates for that reason. A fleet with full telemetry records its minor bumps whether or not anyone calls the police.

One side argues that comparing a telemetry-complete fleet count with a police-reported human count makes the fleet look worse than it is. The other argues that minor crashes are exactly what a pilot should be judged on, and that adjusting the fleet's count downward invites flattering choices. Both are serious. The data team's job is to say which comparison a number makes, not to settle it.

Part 3 · covers the feedback loop and the cost of being wrong

A fleet that writes its own data, and a mistake you cannot refund

6 min live

A sales dashboard reads the world without changing it: refreshing it does not change what the till records. A fleet is not like that. Its takeover policy, its upload triggers and its next model decide which miles become data, so the data it learns from is partly a record of its own choices. And when the analysis is wrong, the cost is not a refund and a corrected rerun. It is a gap in a safety argument.

LiveThe feedback loop: the fleet's policy is in the data3 min▶

A warehouse metric can drift because the business changes, but recording a sale does not depend on last month's dashboard. On a fleet, it does. The takeover policy decides when a safety driver steps in, the upload triggers decide which moments are kept, and both are set by people reading last month's numbers. Change either and the data changes, with the road unchanged.

Lanehart's constructed anti-lever makes it concrete. Three takeover policies drive the same supervised miles. The cautious one logs 0.987 disengagements per 100,000 miles, the standard 0.193, the loose 0.027. Rank by "fewest disengagements" and you pick loose, which lets 21 more conflicts become crashes and raises the true rate from 192.24 to 206.24 per 100 million. Cautious and standard have identical true safety and a 5x difference in the score. The metric measured the policy.

Shadow mode is the honest version of the loop: the new software runs alongside without driving, and the team compares what it would have done with what happened. Even then, which moments get uploaded for comparison is a trigger someone chose. Practitioner session 9 builds the loop.

Real world

California's regulation, Title 13, section 227.50, defines a disengagement as a deactivation of the autonomous mode when a failure of the technology is detected or when safe operation requires the test driver to take manual control, and requires the annual report to say who initiated it. Who decides that safe operation requires a takeover is the operator's policy. NHTSA's Standing General Order shows the same mechanism at a different edge: an ADS crash is reportable if the system was in use at any time within 30 seconds of it, so the window is a rule about which events enter the record.

LiveThe cost of being wrong: a refund against a safety case3 min▶

A warehouse error has a ceiling and a messenger. The wrong price ships, a customer complains, finance refunds, the next run is right. The error is visible because the person harmed reports it, and it is bounded by the order value.

A driving-data error has neither. If the tail slices fall out of evaluation, nothing complains: the dashboard gets greener. Lanehart's constructed canon shows the size. With the four rare-scenario classes evaluated, the crash rate is 192.24 per 100 million (upper bound 215.71), worse than the human 185.55. Drop them and the same fleet reads 147.74 (upper 169.04): "proven" better, falsely. The analysis did not get slightly worse; it reversed the verdict.

That is why the industry frames the output as a safety case rather than a report. UL 4600 (reported) describes a safety case as structured claims, argument and evidence across the lifecycle, using safety performance indicators. The data team owns the evidence leaves, and a missing leaf is a hole in the argument, not a footnote.

Self-studyFailures with no fault in them3 min▶

Warehouse quality work hunts for faults: a broken feed, a null, a duplicate. Two safety standards split the problem differently, both at the reported tier and concept level only here. ISO 26262 covers hazards from malfunctions of electrical and electronic systems. ISO 21448, safety of the intended functionality, covers hazards with no fault at all: a sensor that works exactly as designed and still misses, because the specification or the performance limit did not anticipate the situation.

The second kind is the one the data team is uniquely placed to find, and the one a fault-hunting playbook will never flag. Every component is green; the child behind the van is still missed. The evidence for it lives in scenario slices, not in pipeline health checks.

Part 4 · build-along

Put one warehouse KPI through the five gates

17 min hands on

No code. Lanehart's analyst was asked to port "crashes this month, by city" to the fleet. Take that KPI, or one from your own team, and pass it through the five mechanisms in order. Each gate either adds a requirement to the definition or tells you the KPI cannot be built as stated. The answers below are a constructed worked example; your answers will differ.

Gate 1, the source: name the clock

Write which system decides that a crash happened and when: a collision sensor, a safety driver's report, a police report. If two of them disagree by a few seconds, which wins? For "crashes by city", also ask which positioning stamp places the crash in a city.

Gate 2, the identity: name the unit

Is the KPI counting crashes, vehicles involved, or actors struck? Each comes from a different inferred key. Write down what happens to the count if the tracker or the context tagger is updated next month.

Gate 3, the ground truth: name the denominator and the rarity

A monthly count has no miles behind it. Rewrite it as a rate per 100 million miles with a bound, and note how few events a month will contain. If the expected count is near zero, say the KPI cannot rank cities at all.

Gate 4, the loop: pin the policy

List the takeover policy, upload triggers and reporting window in force for the month. If any changed, write "not comparable with last month" next to the chart. Never rank teams or cities by disengagements.

Gate 5, the cost: name the missing leaf

Which rare situations does the KPI leave out, and would the verdict flip if they were added? On Lanehart's constructed numbers it does: 147.74 without the tail, 192.24 with it, against a human 185.55.

Write the rewritten definition in one line

Compare yours with the yellow line in the figure. Differences are fine; a definition that skipped a gate is not.

The habit to keep. Before porting any warehouse metric to driving data, ask five questions in order: whose clock, whose key, whose label and how rare, whose policy shaped the log, and what a miss costs. If a gate has no answer, the metric is not ready, however familiar it looks.

Check yourself

Three questions

An analyst joins every camera frame to the lidar sweep with the nearest timestamp, as she would join two event tables. Why is that risky on a drive log?

Synchronised clocks are not enough when the stamps mark different events. C has the rates backwards: the cameras run at 12 Hz and the lidar at 20 Hz. The fix is a join that respects what each stamp means; practitioner session 1 builds it.

Lanehart (constructed) compares three takeover policies. Which reading of "fewest disengagements per 100,000 miles" is right?

The fleet's own policy decides what gets logged, so ranking by the score rewards the policy that intervenes least. Refreshing it more often, as in C, only repeats the mistake faster.

The four rare-scenario slices are dropped from Lanehart's evaluation (constructed). What happens to the safety case?

Rare in miles is not small in risk: the tail adds 44.50 per 100 million in the canon run. Removing evidence did not make the case cautious, it made it wrong, and nothing in the dashboard complains.
