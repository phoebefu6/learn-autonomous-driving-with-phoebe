Simulation against real miles

Nobody can drive enough miles to prove a fatality rate, so the rare situations get tested in simulation. That moves the question rather than answering it: which part of a safety number came from the road, which part came from a simulator, and how far can the simulator be trusted? Two kinds of simulation answer different questions, and the gap between simulated and real behaviour hides inside one factor of the tail rate. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies every fleet number; only the human baselines are real.

Part 0

Lanehart's simulation report, and the one question it could not answer

Lanehart is a constructed robotaxi pilot. Its simulation team brought a tidy report to the safety review: the new planner had been run through thousands of synthetic scenarios, including a pedestrian stepping out from behind a parked van. The safety lead asked two things. How often does that situation happen on our own roads? And would the car really fail in it as rarely as the simulator says? The first is a question about real miles. The second is a question about the simulator. A safety number needs both answers, and they come from different places.

LiveWhy simulation is not optional3 min▶

Session 3 on the leader track did the arithmetic: to show with 95% confidence that a fleet's fatality rate is at most the human rate, with zero fatal crashes, takes about 225.2 million miles at the 2022 US rate of 1.33 per 100 million. Estimating the rate precisely, or showing it is better by a margin, takes billions. The rare scenarios that cause those crashes are rarer still per mile.

Real world

RAND's "Driving to Safety" (Kalra and Paddock, 2016) put the numbers at 275 million failure-free miles to demonstrate a fatality rate of at most 1.09 per 100 million, about 8.8 billion miles to estimate it within 20%, and 11 billion miles to show it is 20% better, which the report puts at about 500 years for a 100-vehicle fleet driving around the clock. Its conclusion: developers cannot simply drive their way to safety, and other methods, among them simulation, scenario testing and staged deployment, are needed.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Log replay. Feeding recorded sensor frames from real drives back into a new version of the software, and watching what it decides.
  • Open loop. The world does not respond to the software's decisions; it plays as recorded.
  • Synthetic scenario. A generated situation (actors, road, timing) with rendered or modelled sensor input, where other actors react.
  • Closed loop. The software's decisions change what happens next, and the simulation follows.
  • Encounter rate. How often a situation occurs per 100 million miles of real driving.
  • Failure probability. The chance the car fails, given it is in that situation.
  • Sim-to-real gap. Any difference between how the car behaves in simulation and how it would behave on the road in the same situation.
Part 1 · covers log replay vs synthetic scenarios

Two kinds of simulation, two different promises

15 min live

"We tested it in simulation" can mean two very different things. Log replay re-runs real recorded drives through new software: real sensors, real people, real oddities, but the recording cannot react. Synthetic scenarios build the situation from scratch: the other car reacts to yours, and the rare case can be generated a thousand times, but every sensor reading and every reaction is a model somebody wrote. Each stops being evidence at a different place.

LiveWhat log replay can and cannot tell you5 min▶

Log replay is the most honest simulation there is, up to a point. The camera frames, lidar sweeps and radar returns are the ones the car really recorded, with the glare, the dirty lens and the pedestrian who hesitated. Feed them to a new perception model and you learn exactly how it would have seen that moment. For perception, that is close to a real measurement.

The point is divergence. The recording holds what the logged car did and how everyone else responded to it. The moment the new planner brakes earlier or changes lane, the other actors on the tape keep doing what they did in response to the old car. The car behind does not slow down for the new brake. From that instant, the outcome of the replay is not a measurement of anything. It is still useful (did the planner choose sensibly at the divergence?), but a "no collision" after divergence is not evidence of safety.

LiveWhat a synthetic scenario buys, and what it costs5 min▶

A synthetic scenario is closed loop: the cut-in car reacts to yours, so outcomes after a new decision mean something. And it is generative: the rare situation that a fleet meets a few thousand times per 100 million miles can be set up on demand, with its parameters swept (gap, speed, timing, occlusion) to find where the car starts to fail.

The cost is that every input is a model. The camera image is rendered, not seen. The lidar returns come from a sensor model. The cut-in driver's reaction comes from a behaviour model, which is only as good as the real driving it was fitted to. And the scenario exists only because somebody thought to write it. Each of those is a place where the simulated car can do better than the real one would.

Self-studyWhy teams run both, and still need real miles4 min▶

The two kinds cover each other's weak side. Replay keeps perception honest against real sensor data; synthetic scenarios test decisions in closed loop and reach the tail. A common pattern is to mine real logs for a rare situation (practitioner session 3), replay it for perception, then rebuild it synthetically so the planner can be tested with the other actors reacting and the parameters swept.

Neither replaces the road for one job: saying how often a situation happens. A simulator can tell you what the car does in a cut-in; only logged miles can tell you how many cut-ins a fleet meets. That split is Part 2.

Part 2 · covers the sim-to-real gap

The tail rate is a product, and the gap hides in one factor

10 min live

For one rare situation, the crash rate it contributes is how often the situation happens times how often the car fails in it. Lanehart's constructed occluded-pedestrian class: 4,000 encounters per 100 million miles, times a failure probability of 0.003, gives 12 crashes per 100 million miles. The first factor is a count from real logs. The second is what simulation and scenario testing estimate. Any sim-to-real gap lands in the second factor, and it almost always pushes in the flattering direction.

LiveThe product, one factor at a time4 min▶

Lanehart's four constructed rare classes, each as encounters per 100 million miles times failure probability per encounter: pedestrian stepping out from behind a parked van, 4,000 x 0.003 = 12; wrong-way vehicle on a divided road, 300 x 0.02 = 6; debris in lane at highway speed, 2,500 x 0.004 = 10; emergency vehicle crossing a junction, 1,200 x 0.015 = 18. The true tail is 46 crashes per 100 million miles.

The encounter rates are counts over logged miles, so they carry ordinary sampling error that session 7 knows how to bound. The failure probabilities cannot be read off the road: Lanehart meets the occluded pedestrian a few thousand times per 100 million miles and fails in it a handful of times. So they come from simulation and scenario testing. Even there the plain approach struggles: in the constructed run, 2,000 straightforward simulated tries of the occluded pedestrian produced exactly 1 failure. Session 8 fixes that with importance sampling.

LiveThree leaks, and which way they push4 min▶
  • Sensor model. A rendered pedestrian behind a rendered van is usually cleaner than a real one: no glare, no rain on the lens, no half-reflective jacket. If perception does better on clean input, the simulated p is too small.
  • Behaviour model. The pedestrian steps out the way the model says people step out. Real people hesitate, run, turn back. If the model's people are more predictable than real ones, p is too small again.
  • Scenario coverage. A situation nobody wrote is never simulated, so its contribution to the tail is zero by omission. That is practitioner session 4's break button arriving through the simulator: drop the tail slices and Lanehart's matched rate of 147.74 looks "proven" (upper bound 169.04 against 185.55).

How big the gap is has no general answer in the sources this session uses; it is specific to a simulator, a sensor model and a scenario. That is the honest position, and it is why the gap has to be measured case by case: replay real logged instances of a scenario and compare with its synthetic twin.

Self-studyAccelerated evaluation, and the hinge it turns on4 min▶

Simulation does not only make rare cases available; it can make them common on purpose and then correct for it. That is the idea session 8 builds: sample the dangerous versions more often, then reweight every result back to how often it happens in real driving.

Real world

Zhao et al. (2017), in IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Transportation Systems, modelled lane-change cut-ins from naturalistic driving data, skewed the statistics toward riskier behaviour (choosing the skew with the cross-entropy method), and reweighted the estimates to real-world rates. The authors report acceleration of 2,000 to 20,000 times: about 1,000 simulated miles exposing the vehicle to challenges that take 2 to 20 million real miles to meet.

Notice the hinge. The reweighting needs the real-world distribution of cut-ins, which came from recorded driving. Accelerated evaluation multiplies the value of real miles; it does not remove the need for them. If the real distribution is wrong, the reweighted answer is wrong by exactly as much.

Self-studyWhat the standards ask for, reported3 min▶

Two standards frame where simulation evidence fits, both reported here at concept level only because the full texts are paid. ISO 21448 (safety of the intended functionality) covers hazards with no fault at all: a sensor that works as designed and still misses, which is exactly the case a too-clean sensor model under-tests. UL 4600 asks for a safety case, a structured set of claims, arguments and evidence; a simulation result enters it as evidence, together with an argument for why the simulator is valid for that claim. Neither standard quotes a number for the sim-to-real gap.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the tail table and break it with an optimistic simulator

17 min hands on

Put Lanehart's four constructed classes in a pandas table, label which column came from logs and which from simulation, then apply a sim-to-real gap to the simulated column and watch the verdict move. Every number here is constructed arithmetic on Lanehart's parameters, compared with the one real baseline: 185.55 police-reported crashes per 100 million miles, derived from NHTSA's 2022 figures. Change the gap factors and your numbers will differ.

Make the scenario table, and say where each column came from

Four constructed classes. Name the columns by their source: encounters come from logged miles, failure probability from the simulator. The sum should be 46.

pythonimport pandas as pd t = pd.DataFrame({ "scenario": ["occluded pedestrian", "wrong-way vehicle", "debris in lane", "emergency vehicle"], "enc_per_100M": [4000, 300, 2500, 1200], # from logged miles "p_sim": [0.003, 0.02, 0.004, 0.015], # from simulation }) t["rate"] = t.enc_per_100M * t.p_sim t, t.rate.sum()

Add the tail to the road-measured rate

Lanehart's exposure-matched crash rate (leader session 4) is 147.74 per 100 million miles (constructed). Add the tail and compare with the human baseline. You should get 193.74 against 185.55: on these constructed numbers, worse than humans.

pythonMATCHED, HUMAN = 147.74, 185.55 def verdict(tail): total = MATCHED + tail return round(total, 2), "worse" if total >= HUMAN else "looks better" verdict(t.rate.sum())

Apply a gap to one class

Suppose the rendered pedestrian is cleaner than a real one and the simulator reports half the true failure probability for that class. The tail falls to 40, the total to 187.74. Still worse, by a margin of a little over 2.

pythongap = {"occluded pedestrian": 0.5} t["p_reported"] = t.p_sim * t.scenario.map(gap).fillna(1.0) tail = (t.enc_per_100M * t.p_reported).sum() tail, verdict(tail)

Make the simulator optimistic everywhere

Set every factor to 0.5. The tail falls to 23 and the total to 170.74, below 185.55. The car did not change. Only the simulator's honesty did.

pythontail = (t.enc_per_100M * t.p_sim * 0.5).sum() tail, verdict(tail)

Break it on purpose: a scenario nobody built

Drop the emergency vehicle class, as if no one had written that scenario. The tail falls to 28 and the total to 175.74: "better", by omission. A missing row raises no error.

pythonkept = t[t.scenario != "emergency vehicle"] tail = kept.rate.sum() tail, verdict(tail)

Write the claim card

For each class, one line: where the encounter rate came from (which logged miles), which simulator produced p, and what check compares that p with real logged instances of the scenario. A class with no such check gets a flag, not a number.

The habit to keep. Whenever a safety number contains simulation, split it into the factor that came from real miles and the factor that came from the simulator, and write down how the simulator was checked against real instances of that scenario. A simulator that is never compared with the road will look better every year, because every leak points the same way.

Check yourself

Three questions

A new planner, run on a replayed log, brakes two seconds earlier than the logged car did and no collision follows. What does the replay show?

Log replay is open loop. Real sensor input makes the moment of the decision real; what happens after the car acts differently is not on the tape. Perception results before divergence remain useful.

In "tail rate = encounters per 100M miles x failure probability", which factor has to come from real driving?

A simulator can set up a cut-in a thousand times but cannot say how many cut-ins a fleet meets. Failures are too rare on the road to estimate p directly, which is why simulation exists at all.

On Lanehart's constructed numbers, the matched rate plus the true tail is 193.74 against a human 185.55. A simulator that reports every failure probability at half its true value gives 170.74. What changed?

The sim-to-real gap enters one factor of the product and scales the tail directly. Real encounter rates do not protect the result if the failure probability beside them is flattering.
