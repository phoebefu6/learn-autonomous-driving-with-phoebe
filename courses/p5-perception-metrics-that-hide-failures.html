Perception metrics that hide failures

A perception team reports one recall number for pedestrians, and the number goes up. That number is an average over every labelled pedestrian in the evaluation set, and most of them are close, in daylight and fully visible. The pedestrians the car most needs to see early are a small share of the set, so they barely move the average. Slice recall by distance band and by scenario and the same release can be better at the easy cases and worse at the hard ones. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies the evaluation set; every count on the page is constructed.

Lanehart's release note said "recall up"

The evaluation set is constructed too: 10,000 labelled pedestrians, each tagged with how far away it was and what the scene was like. Nobody has lied. The question for tonight is what that one number is an average of, and what it is hiding.

LiveRecall, said plainly3 min▶

Recall is the share of real objects the detector found. Take every labelled pedestrian in the evaluation set, ask whether a detection was matched to it, and divide the matches by the pedestrians. A missed pedestrian lowers recall; a false alarm does not touch it (that is precision, the other half).

For a car, a missed pedestrian is the failure that matters first, which is why recall is the headline. The trap is the word "every": the answer depends entirely on which pedestrians happen to be in the set.

Real world

Ground truth only exists where somebody labelled it. The public multi-sensor research dataset from session 1 labels keyframes at 2 Hz, 40,000 of them, and the authors report that those labels interpolate well to 10 or 20 Hz. So an evaluation set is a sample of labelled moments, and its mix of near and far, clear and hidden, was decided by what was driven and what was labelled, not by what the car will meet.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Headline metric. One number over the whole evaluation set.
  • Slice. A subset defined by a condition (distance band, light, weather, occlusion) and scored on its own.
  • Distance band. A slice by range from the car, with edges chosen in metres.
  • Occluded. Partly hidden behind something, here a parked vehicle; the label still exists, the pixels and points are fewer.
  • Match. The rule that pairs a detection with a labelled object. Its threshold is a choice, and recall moves when it moves.
  • Constructed. The 10,000 pedestrians and both models' hits are invented for teaching; your numbers will differ.
Part 1 · covers the headline as a weighted average, distance bands

One number, four distance bands

15 min live

Put the 10,000 constructed pedestrians on the road in front of the car and group them by distance. Sixty percent stand within 30 m, five percent stand beyond 80 m. The current model finds 94.8% of the near ones and 54.2% of the far ones. The headline, 90.0%, sits almost on top of the near band, because the near band is most of the set.

LiveThe headline is a weighted average5 min▶

Write the headline out and the hiding place is visible. Overall recall is each band's recall times that band's share of the set, added up:

0.60 x 94.8 + 0.25 x 90.9 + 0.10 x 77.3 + 0.05 x 54.2 = 90.0

The far band carries a weight of 0.05. If its recall fell by 10 points, the headline would fall by half a point, which is inside the wobble a team expects between two training runs. The near band carries 0.60: a 1 point gain there lifts the headline by 0.6. Any change that trades far pedestrians for near ones looks like progress.

The weights are not a law of nature. They are the mix of the evaluation set, which came from what was driven and what was labelled. Change the set and the same model gets a different headline.

LiveWhy distance is the first slice5 min▶

A pedestrian twice as far away covers far fewer camera pixels and returns far fewer lidar points. The paper behind the public multi-sensor research dataset describes its lidar range as typically 50 to 150 m and radar to about 200 m. So the far band is where the sensors are thinnest, and it is also where a moving car still has room to slow down gently. A miss at 120 m is a lost head start; a miss at 20 m is a miss that never gets a second look.

Slicing by distance is not a statistical refinement. It asks the question the planner asks: at what range can I rely on this detector?

Real world

ISO 21448 (safety of the intended functionality, reported, concepts only) names hazards that arise with no fault at all, from performance limits of a system working as designed. A detector that misses half the pedestrians beyond 80 m has no broken part. Only a slice shows that limit; the headline averages it away.

Self-studyChoosing band edges4 min▶

Lanehart's edges (30, 50, 80, 150 m) are constructed. Pick yours from two facts you can defend: where the sensors change character (the reported 50 to 150 m lidar range is one such edge) and the distances the planner cares about at the speeds your car drives. Write the reason beside each edge.

Keep the edges fixed between releases. A band that moves every time someone retrains is a slice nobody can compare. And keep the last band bounded: pedestrians labelled beyond the sensor's range belong in their own row, not quietly averaged into the far band.

LiveRead the candidate off the table4 min▶
  • Headline: 90.0% to 91.3%. The intervals do not overlap, so the rise is real. It is also the least useful row.
  • Near bands: 0-30 m and 30-50 m both rise clearly. The candidate has got better at the pedestrians the old model already found.
  • Beyond 80 m: 54.2% to 41.6% on 500 pedestrians; intervals 49.8 to 58.5% against 37.4 to 46.0%. Real, and in the wrong direction.
  • Partly occluded: 51.3% to 39.7% on 300; also clear of noise.
  • Rain: 79.2% to 79.4%. No change you can claim either way.

The pattern is common enough to name: tuning that sharpens confident detections can drop the faint ones. The headline rewards it, because faint pedestrians are a small share of the set.

LiveWhen a slice is too small to judge3 min▶

Slice far enough and the cells empty out. Only 15 of the constructed pedestrians are both beyond 80 m and partly occluded. The current model found 5 (33.3%), the candidate 3 (20.0%). That looks like a 13 point fall, but the 95% intervals are 15.2 to 58.3% and 7.0 to 45.2%: 15 pedestrians cannot tell 20% from 45%.

So print the interval beside every slice, and treat a small cell as a request for more labelled examples of that kind, not as a verdict. That request is how a slice table feeds practitioner session 3 (mining the fleet for those moments) and session 4 (keeping them in evaluation).

Self-studyA sharper comparison, and where general metrics live4 min▶

Both models were scored on the same pedestrians, so the comparison is paired: count the pedestrians the current model found and the candidate missed, and the reverse. A paired test is sharper than checking whether two intervals overlap; overlap is the cautious first pass, and it errs towards "within noise".

Precision, average precision, calibration and the general case against a single score are owned by learn model evaluation. The side taken here is narrower: for a perception model, the slices are distance bands and driving scenarios, and their edges come from the sensor and the road.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the slice table in pandas

17 min hands on

Rebuild the widget from labelled rows and print the same table. The counts below are the constructed set; in real work the rows come from matching detections to labels, one row per labelled object. The figure is the plan: one row per pedestrian, two tags, one group-by, an interval and a flag.

Build the constructed evaluation set

Four distance bands by four scenarios, the number of labelled pedestrians in each cell, and how many each model found. Expand it to one row per pedestrian, which is the shape your real matched table will have.

pythonimport math import pandas as pd bands = ["0-30 m", "30-50 m", "50-80 m", "80-150 m"] scen = ["clear", "night", "rain", "occluded"] n = [[4800, 720, 300, 180], [2000, 300, 125, 75], [800, 120, 50, 30], [400, 60, 25, 15]] hit_a = [[4704, 635, 250, 97], [1880, 254, 100, 39], [640, 86, 34, 13], [224, 30, 12, 5]] hit_b = [[4776, 666, 251, 75], [1950, 272, 102, 31], [664, 93, 35, 10], [172, 24, 9, 3]] rows = [] for i, b in enumerate(bands): for j, s in enumerate(scen): for k in range(n[i][j]): rows.append((b, s, k < hit_a[i][j], k < hit_b[i][j])) gt = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["band", "scenario", "hit_a", "hit_b"]) len(gt)

Print the headline first

You should see 0.9003 for the current model and 0.9133 for the candidate: the release note's numbers.

pythongt[["hit_a", "hit_b"]].mean().round(4)

Slice by band, then by scenario

One function, two calls. Keep sort=False so the bands stay in distance order. The 80-150 m row should read 0.542, 0.416, -0.126 and the occluded row 0.513, 0.397, -0.117.

pythondef slices(df, by): t = df.groupby(by, sort=False).agg(n=("hit_a", "size"), recall_a=("hit_a", "mean"), recall_b=("hit_b", "mean")) t["change"] = t.recall_b - t.recall_a return t.round(3) slices(gt, "band") slices(gt, "scenario")

Add the interval and the read

A Wilson interval behaves well near 0% and 100% and on small slices. Mark a slice "fell" only when the candidate's whole interval sits below the current one. On the constructed set, 80-150 m and occluded fall; rain and 50-80 m stay within noise.

pythondef wilson(k, m, z=1.96): p = k / m d = 1 + z * z / m c = (p + z * z / (2 * m)) / d h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / m + z * z / (4 * m * m)) / d return c - h, c + h def read(df, by): g = df.groupby(by, sort=False).agg(n=("hit_a", "size"), a=("hit_a", "sum"), b=("hit_b", "sum")) out = [] for key, r in g.iterrows(): la, ha = wilson(r.a, r.n) lb, hb = wilson(r.b, r.n) verdict = "rose" if lb > ha else "fell" if hb < la else "within noise" out.append((key, r.n, round(r.a / r.n, 3), round(r.b / r.n, 3), verdict)) return pd.DataFrame(out, columns=["slice", "n", "recall_a", "recall_b", "read"]) read(gt, "band")

Cross the slices and flag the small cells

Group by band and scenario together. Three cells hold fewer than 50 pedestrians; the smallest, far and occluded, has 15. Every one of them reads "within noise", which is the honest answer and a to-do for the data team.

pythoncells = read(gt, ["band", "scenario"]) cells[cells.n < 50]

Write the release rule down

One line your team agrees before the next candidate arrives: which slices are gates, what counts as "fell", and what happens when a gate slice is too small to call. A rule written after seeing the numbers tends to bend towards the release.

The habit to keep. Never report a perception headline alone. Report it beside the slice table, distance bands first, each slice with its count and its interval, and decide in advance which slices can block a release. If the headline went up, look first for the slice that went down.

Check yourself

Three questions

On the constructed set, recall beyond 80 m falls by 12.6 points, yet headline recall rises. How can both be true?

Same 10,000 pedestrians, same matching. The weights are the set's mix: 0.05 x 12.6 is about 0.6, which the near-band gains outweigh. The far band sits inside the reported 50 to 150 m lidar range.

Only 15 pedestrians are both beyond 80 m and partly occluded. The current model found 5, the candidate 3. What should the slice table say?

Fifteen examples cannot tell 20% from 45%. Dropping the cell hides that the set has almost none of the hardest case; keeping it with its interval turns it into a mining task.

Where should distance band edges come from?

Equal-count bands put most edges inside the near range and move every time the set changes, so no two releases are comparable. Edges chosen after seeing results are tuning, not measurement.

Before session 6

Homework

Thirty minutes, in your own notebook.

Change the mix, not the model

Keep both models' slice recalls and reweight the bands to a mix you choose, for example equal shares. Recompute both headlines. Does the candidate still win? Your numbers will differ with your mix; write the mix down before you compute.

Add a third slice dimension

Add a column of your own (pedestrian size, or whether the car was turning) to the constructed rows and slice by it. Count how many cells fall under 50 and say what you would mine for first.

Find one headline you report today

In any model or dashboard you own, pick one single-number metric. Write down the two slices that matter most to the people who act on it, and check whether anyone has ever looked at them.
