Learn Autonomous Driving with Phoebe · Practitioner session 10 of 10

The prove-it-safe bench

Nine sessions took a safety claim apart one piece at a time. This one runs the whole ladder on Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city: the naive count of fatal-crash-free miles, exact Poisson bounds, a comparison matched to the human driver's mix of roads, and an importance-sampled rate for the rare scenarios the fleet almost never meets. Then two traps. One scores the fleet by disengagements per mile and picks the worse takeover policy. The other drops the rare-scenario slices from evaluation and turns a losing claim green. The human baselines are real NHTSA rates; every fleet number is generated by a seeded engine in your browser and labelled so.

LiveWhat the bench recomputes on every press4 min▶

A seeded generator gives Lanehart's fleet miles in four road contexts (highway 55 percent, urban day 30, night 10, rain 5) and draws crash counts from each context's constructed true rate. It draws the fatal-crash count the same way. The human baseline is real: 1.33 deaths and, derived from the same NHTSA report, 185.55 police-reported crashes per 100 million vehicle miles in 2022. Its split across contexts is constructed, scaled so the human mix averages exactly 185.55.

The rungs: the naive count; the exact Poisson upper bound (gamma form) on the fatal rate and on the all-crash rate; the exposure-matched rate, weighting each context by the human mix, with the Fay and Feuer gamma bound; and the four rare scenario classes scored in a simulator by importance sampling, then added. A claim is "proven better" only when its 95 percent upper bound sits below the human rate. Modelled, and labelled so on the widget: every fleet mile, rate and scenario.

Lanehart is worse in rain and drives 5 percent of its miles there, against 10 percent for the human mix. That is the exposure mismatch in one row.
  • Set the miles to 60 million. Own mix 136.67, upper 164.22, still proven; matched 168.23, upper 205.62, not proven. With fewer rain and night miles behind it, the match widens the bound past the human line: the verdict flips on exposure alone.

  • Try seeds 1, 7 and 42. The counts move, because they are draws. Quote seed-1016 numbers and say "your numbers move with the seed". What holds across seeds is the shape: the matched rate sits above the own-mix rate, and adding the tail pushes the claim toward or over the human line.
Self-studyWhy the matched bound is wider, not just higher4 min▶

The own-mix bound treats 180 crashes over 150 million miles as one Poisson count. The matched rate is a weighted sum of four context rates, and the weights follow the human mix, so rain gets weight 0.10 although Lanehart drove only 7.5 million miles there. Thin strata with large weights carry most of the variance. The Fay and Feuer (1997) gamma interval handles that; the reference script checks its one-sided 95 percent coverage by simulation at 0.9575.

This is the mechanism behind every "safer than humans" headline that compares a fleet's easy miles with all human miles. Matching is not a correction you can skip because the point estimate barely moved; it changes the uncertainty.

Self-studyWhy the tail needs a sampler, not more miles4 min▶

The four constructed rare classes have a true combined rate of 46 crashes per 100 million miles: a pedestrian stepping out from behind a parked van (12), a wrong-way vehicle (6), debris at highway speed (10), an emergency vehicle crossing a junction (18). Plain Monte Carlo with 2,000 simulated encounters per class saw the pedestrian failure once and estimated 34.95 in total; importance sampling estimated 44.50.

Over 200 repeated runs at that budget the plain estimate has a standard deviation of 7.251 and the importance-sampled one 1.404, about 5.2 times tighter, with a mean of 46.05 against the true 46. At 500 encounters, plain Monte Carlo saw zero failures in at least one class in 35 percent of runs. Zhao and colleagues (2017) report accelerations of 2,000 to 20,000 times with the same idea on real cut-in data.

Part 2 · the anti-lever and the break button

A metric that rewards the worse policy, and a button that hides the tail

14 min hands on

Disengagements are reported because a regulation asks for them, and they are easy to rank. The California regulation defines one as the autonomous mode switching off when the technology fails or when safe operation requires the test driver to take over. Who decides "requires" is the operator's takeover policy. The figure draws what that does to the number.

LivePress the anti-lever4 min▶

The widget scores the three constructed takeover policies by disengagements per 100,000 miles and picks the lowest: loose, at 0.027. It then prints the true rate under each policy: 192.24 for cautious and standard, 206.24 for loose, 14.00 per 100 million worse. The metric chose the policy with 21 extra crashes.

Go the other way too. Cautious against standard: 0.987 against 0.193 disengagements, five times as many, with identical safety. A team that tightens its policy looks worse on the leaderboard and is not. Disengagements are a fine operational signal about a test programme; they are not a safety rate. The California regulation asks who initiated each disengagement for exactly this reason.

LivePress the break button4 min▶

The break button removes the four rare-scenario slices from evaluation and reruns the verdict. The rate falls back to the matched 147.74, upper 169.04, under the human 185.55: "proven better". The widget prints that verdict and names why it is false: the slices that were dropped carried 44.50 crashes per 100 million.

This is the break button's job: if hiding the tail did not change the verdict, the tail would not be in the claim at all. Because it does, the bench proves it is measuring the tail. Practitioner session 4 is about keeping those slices in evaluation even when they are a rounding error in the training set.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree4 min▶

Is a fleet's crash count comparable with police-reported human crashes at all? A fleet with full telemetry records minor contacts a human driver would never report, which inflates its count; human baselines undercount, which deflates theirs. RAND reports separate "reported" and "estimated total" human rates for this reason. Some argue for comparing only serious injuries and deaths, where reporting is complete but events are rarer still; others for crash-type matching. The bench uses police-reported crashes because the NHTSA figure exists at source; treat the comparison as a lower bar, not a settled one.

Part 3 · build-along

Write the claim the bench supports

11 min hands on

Open the browser console on this page. The engine is loaded as AV. Reproduce the canon in four lines, then write a five-line claim from your own runs. The figure is the card to fill.

Run the canon ladder

You should see 0, 2.00, 120.00, 147.74 and 192.24, the canon. The verdict strings are the engine's own.

consolevar L = AV.ladder({}); [L.fatal.k, L.fatal.upper.toFixed(2), L.own.point.toFixed(2), L.matched.point.toFixed(2), L.withTail.point.toFixed(2), L.withTail.verdict]

Check the rule of three by hand

With zero events the exact bound is -ln(0.05) = 2.996, about 3. Divide by the miles in hundreds of millions and you get the fatal bound: 2.996 / 1.5 = 2.00. The miles needed are 2.996 / 1.33 hundred million, 225.2 million.

console[AV.poissonUpper(0, 0.95), AV.poissonUpper(0, 0.95) / 1.5, L.fatal.milesNeeded]

Read the sampler against plain Monte Carlo

Standard deviations over 200 runs: 7.251 plain, 1.404 importance-sampled. The importance-sampled mean, 46.05, sits on the constructed truth of 46.

console[L.variance.mcSD, L.variance.isSD, L.variance.isMean, L.variance.trueTotal]

Plant a fault and watch the engine catch it

The engine can re-run its own checks with a fault planted back in the real code path. Each of the three faults fails at least one check.

consoleAV.FAULTS.map(function (f) { return f + ": " + AV.selfTest(f).pass; })

Write the five lines

Exposure, bound, match, tail, policy, as in the figure, filled from your own runs. For line 4, name every scenario class you did not test. If that list is long, line 4 is the finding.

The habit to keep. Never sign off a "safer than humans" number without its exposure, its bound, its match to the comparison's mix of roads, its tail and the metric it refused to use. And never trust a bench you have not broken: if hiding the tail does not change the verdict, the tail was never in the claim.

Check yourself

Three questions

Lanehart's own-mix crash rate is 120.00 per 100 million miles, upper bound 135.79, against a human 185.55. Matched to the human mix of roads it is 147.74. Why does the match raise it?

Same crashes, same miles, different weights. The own-mix rate answers "how safe on the roads it chose"; the matched rate answers "how safe on the roads people drive". Only the second can be compared with 185.55.

Ranked by disengagements per 100,000 miles, the loose takeover policy wins (0.027 against 0.193 and 0.987). What does the bench say?

Disengagements depend on when the operator chooses to take over. Cautious and standard tie on safety with five times different counts; loose looks best and is worst.

What does the break button (drop the rare-scenario slices) prove?

The car is unchanged between the two verdicts; only the evaluation changed. The sampler's mean over 200 runs (46.05) sits on the constructed truth of 46, so C is not the reason.

After the track

Homework

About an hour, partly in the console here and partly on something you own.

Find the miles where the tail stops mattering

Run the ladder at more miles in the console (for example AV.ladder({ miles: 2e9 })). Does the claim with the tail ever turn green? Write down why more fleet miles cannot fix a tail whose rate is too high. Your numbers will differ by seed.

Break your own evaluation

Take a model you own and remove its rarest evaluation slice. If the headline number barely moves, write down whether that slice was ever large enough in your evaluation set to move it.

Write the five lines for a real claim

Exposure, bound, match, tail, policy. Hand it to whoever signs off the claim and ask which line they would have asked for first.
