Anatomy of a drive log

A warehouse table has one row per event and a key that says which. A drive log has frames from cameras, lidar and radar, each sensor ticking on its own clock, and a sparse set of keyframes that somebody paid to label. Before anything can be measured, the frames that belong to one moment have to be found, which is a join on time, and the join most people reach for first is wrong. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies the log; its timing model follows the published design of a public multi-sensor research dataset, and every count on the page is computed in your browser.

Part 0

Lanehart's first query, and why it returned nothing

Lanehart is a constructed robotaxi pilot. Its data analyst, fresh from a warehouse team, was asked for "every camera image with the lidar scan taken at the same moment". She wrote the join she always writes, on equal timestamps, and got zero rows. The cameras and the lidar never stamp the same instant, so equality finds nothing. The fix everyone tries next, the nearest timestamp, returns every row and is wrong for most of them. The ten practitioner sessions build up from exactly this: a log you can trust to put one moment together.

LiveWhat is in a drive log4 min▶

Sensor frames. Images from each camera, lidar point clouds from each sweep, radar returns. Each frame carries a timestamp, and the paper behind the public dataset is precise about which instant it means: an image is stamped at its exposure trigger time, a lidar scan at the moment its full rotation completes.

Vehicle state. Position and orientation from localisation, plus the vehicle's own bus signals: speed, acceleration, steering angle. The same paper reports localisation errors of 10 cm or less from lidar and odometry, and releases the raw bus data alongside.

Labels. Boxes and attributes on a sample of frames, the keyframes. In the public dataset they are sampled at 2 Hz, and the authors report that labels at that rate interpolate well to 10 or 20 Hz. Most frames are never labelled by a person.

Real world

The public dataset's paper describes 1,000 scenes of about 20 seconds each, six cameras, one 32-beam lidar and five radars giving 360-degree coverage, and 40,000 labelled keyframes. Its own footnote names the trap this session is about: 12 camera exposures are spread across 20 lidar scans each second, "so not all lidar scans have a corresponding camera frame".

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Frame. One capture from one sensor at one instant.
  • Sweep or scan. One full rotation of a spinning lidar; at 20 Hz, 50 ms.
  • Exposure trigger. The instant a camera starts capturing; its timestamp.
  • Keyframe. A frame chosen for labelling.
  • As-of join. A join that matches each row to the nearest row before, after or around it in time, within a tolerance, instead of on equality.
  • Constructed. Lanehart's timing in the widget is a model built on the published design, not a recording.
Part 1 · covers frames, clocks and timestamps

One moment, many clocks

15 min live

Draw half a second of the log and the problem is visible. The lidar stamps a scan every 50 ms, at the end of each rotation. A camera fires when the sweep passes the centre of its view, so a camera facing forward fires at the start of a rotation and one facing back-left fires about 35 ms later. Radar ticks on its own 13 Hz clock. Labels exist only at the two keyframes.

LiveWhich instant each timestamp means5 min▶

The public dataset's paper states the rule: the timestamp of an image is the exposure trigger time, and the timestamp of a lidar scan is when the full rotation of that frame completes. So a lidar scan stamped at 200 ms contains points measured from 150 to 200 ms, and a camera frame stamped at 165 ms that was taken during that rotation belongs to the scan stamped 200, not to the one stamped 150, even though 150 is closer.

Every sensor has such a rule, and it is rarely written in the column name. A warehouse habit is to treat a timestamp as "when the row happened". In a drive log the first question about any timestamp column is: start, middle or end of what?

LiveWhy the clocks are not merged at the source4 min▶

Each sensor has its own physics. A spinning lidar's natural unit is a rotation. A camera's is an exposure, and its rate is a trade between light, motion blur and bandwidth. Radar has its own cycle. The public dataset triggers each camera as the lidar passes the camera's centre, which aligns the two where it matters, and the paper notes that motion compensation is applied using localisation. What it does not do is give every stream one shared tick, because no such tick exists.

So the log keeps every stream at its own rate, and alignment is a decision made at query time. That makes it the data team's decision, and it can be made wrongly.

Self-studyWhat 2 Hz labels mean for everything after4 min▶

At 2 Hz, a 20-second scene has about 40 labelled keyframes against about 240 camera frames per camera. The paper reports that its 2 Hz annotations are robust to interpolation to 10 or 20 Hz, which is why sparse labelling is affordable. Interpolation is a model, though: an object that appears and disappears between two keyframes is never labelled at all.

That is the bridge to practitioner session 2 (labels are bought by the frame) and to the long tail: the frames that matter most are the ones least likely to be keyframes.

Part 2 · covers sensor sync as a join

Synchronising is a join, and the default join is wrong

10 min live

An equality join on timestamps returns nothing, because no two streams share an instant. An as-of join matches each camera frame to a lidar scan near it in time. The direction decides whether the match is the scan the frame was taken in. Take the back-right camera, which fires 15.3 ms into a rotation: the nearest stamp is the previous scan's end, which is the wrong scan.

LiveThe four joins, read off the widget5 min▶
  • Nearest, 60 ms: 28 correct, 44 wrong, 0 unmatched. Every camera that fires before the half-turn snaps to the previous scan. Every row comes back, which is why the error survives code review.
  • Backward, 60 ms: 0 correct, 66 wrong, 6 unmatched. The warehouse habit "latest value as of this time" is exactly backwards for a stamp at the end of a window.
  • Forward, 60 ms: 61 correct, 11 wrong. The 11 are the front camera, which fires exactly on the previous scan's stamp; a tie resolves to the earlier scan.
  • Strictly after, 60 ms: 72 of 72. The rule follows from the timestamp definitions, not from tuning.

Now set the tolerance to 20 ms with "at or after": 24 correct, 11 wrong, 37 unmatched. A tolerance shorter than one rotation silently drops most cameras. Tolerance is a fact about the sensors (one 50 ms rotation), not a knob.

Self-studyWhere this sits next to sensor drift3 min▶

Drift, resampling and clocks on a single device are owned by learn IoT data. The point here is narrower: within one drive log, synchronisation is a join with a direction and a tolerance, and both come from how each sensor defines its timestamp.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the sync join in pandas

17 min hands on

Rebuild the widget's second in pandas and get the same three counts. Run it in a notebook or a scratch folder. The figure is the plan: two tables sorted by time, one as-of join, and the two arguments that decide the answer.

Make the lidar table

Twenty scans in one second, each stamped at the end of its 50 ms rotation. Keep a copy of the stamp as its own column, because the join key is consumed.

pythonimport pandas as pd lidar = pd.DataFrame({"t_ms": [50.0 * (i + 1) for i in range(20)]}) lidar["scan_end"] = lidar["t_ms"]

Make the camera frames, with the truth beside them

Twelve exposures spread over twenty scans, six cameras at their azimuths, each firing as the sweep crosses its centre. The truth column is the end of the rotation the frame was taken in. This is the constructed model behind the widget.

pythonfire = [j * 20 // 12 for j in range(12)] cams = {"front": 0, "front right": 55, "back right": 110, "back": 180, "back left": 250, "front left": 305} rows = [(c, s, 50 * s + az / 360 * 50, 50 * (s + 1)) for c, az in cams.items() for s in fire] frames = pd.DataFrame(rows, columns=["camera", "scan", "t_ms", "truth"]).sort_values("t_ms") fire

Join the nearest way first, and count

You should see 28 correct, 44 wrong and 0 missing, the widget's first line. Note that every frame got a match: nothing in the output warns you.

pythondef score(m): ok = (m.scan_end == m.truth).sum() bad = (m.scan_end.notna() & (m.scan_end != m.truth)).sum() return ok, bad, m.scan_end.isna().sum() score(pd.merge_asof(frames, lidar, on="t_ms", direction="nearest", tolerance=60))

Point it forward, then make it strict

Forward gives 61, 11, 0. Turning off exact matches gives 72, 0, 0. Look at which camera the 11 were: it is the one that fires on the boundary.

pythonscore(pd.merge_asof(frames, lidar, on="t_ms", direction="forward", tolerance=60)) score(pd.merge_asof(frames, lidar, on="t_ms", direction="forward", tolerance=60, allow_exact_matches=False))

Break it on purpose: mixed dtypes

Build the lidar stamps as integers (drop the ".0") and rerun. pandas 2.1 refuses with "incompatible merge keys", float64 against int64. That error is a gift: a database would often cast silently.

pythonlidar_int = pd.DataFrame({"t_ms": [50 * (i + 1) for i in range(20)]}) pd.merge_asof(frames, lidar_int, on="t_ms", direction="forward")

Write the sync rule down

One line per sensor: what its timestamp means (start, end, trigger), its period, and the join that attaches it to the lidar. That line belongs next to the table definition, not in someone's head.

The habit to keep. Before you join two sensor streams, write down what instant each timestamp means. Then choose the direction from those definitions, the tolerance from the sensor period, and test the join against a case where you know the truth. A join that returns every row is not evidence that it is right.

Check yourself

Three questions

On the constructed second, joining each camera frame to the nearest lidar stamp puts only 28 of 72 frames on the right scan. Why?

The timestamps follow a rule; the join ignored it. Shrinking the tolerance makes things worse (at 20 ms, 15 frames go unmatched). Direction comes from the timestamp definitions.

With "first stamp at or after the frame" and a 20 ms tolerance, 37 of 72 frames go unmatched. What is the right fix?

The unmatched frames are real frames from cameras that fire early in the rotation. Dropping them deletes most of the cameras; switching to nearest matches them wrongly.

The public dataset labels keyframes at 2 Hz. What follows for the data team?

The authors report that 2 Hz labels interpolate well to 10 or 20 Hz for objects that persist. Interpolation cannot invent an object nobody labelled, which is why rare, brief events are the expensive ones.

Before session 2

Homework

Thirty minutes, in your own notebook.

Add a radar stream and join it

Radar at 13 Hz, stamped (say) at the middle of its cycle. Decide the direction and tolerance from that definition before you write the join, then check how many radar frames land on each lidar scan. Your numbers will differ with your definition; write it down first.

Find one timestamp column you use today with no definition

In any system you work with, find a time column whose meaning (start, end, insert time, event time) is not written down. Ask its owner, and write the answer next to the schema.
