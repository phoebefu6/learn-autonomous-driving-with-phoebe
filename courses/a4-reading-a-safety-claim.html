Reading a safety claim

Every safety claim is a fraction, and most of the trouble hides in the bottom half. A count of crash reports has no miles under it. A rate on easy miles is compared with a rate on all miles. A falling count of takeovers is read as a safer car when it may only be a looser policy. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies the worked numbers; every Lanehart number is generated, not observed. The human baselines are real and cited.

LiveA claim is a fraction with a comparison word3 min▶

Strip any safety claim down and three parts are left: a numerator (what was counted), a denominator (how much driving it covers) and a comparison (better than whom, on what driving). Leader session 3 was about one more part, how sure you can be given how few events there are. Tonight is about the other three, because they fail more quietly.

A warehouse analyst already knows the shape of the trap: conversion rate by channel, where the channel with the best rate also gets the easiest traffic. Driving has the same trap with higher stakes. Highway miles, city streets at night and rain are different exposures, and a fleet chooses where it drives.

Self-studyThe words that matter tonight3 min▶
  • Numerator. The events counted: crashes, police-reported crashes, injuries, deaths, takeovers. Which one is a choice, and each has its own reporting rule.
  • Exposure. The denominator, usually miles, and its kind: road type, time of day, weather, the operating domain.
  • Exposure mix. The share of miles in each kind of driving. Two fleets with identical risk per context can show different overall rates only because their mixes differ.
  • Exposure-matched rate. Each context's rate re-weighted by the comparison group's mix. Epidemiologists call it a directly standardised rate.
  • Disengagement. The automated mode switching off because a failure was detected or the safety driver took manual control (California's definition).
Part 1 · covers the missing denominator and the exposure mismatch

A count is not a rate, and a rate is not a comparison

15 min live

The first two failures stack. A count with no miles under it cannot be compared with anything. Add the miles and you have a rate, but a rate earned on easy driving still cannot be set against a rate earned on all driving. The figure shows Lanehart's constructed crashes weighted two ways: by the miles Lanehart actually drove, and by the mix of miles human drivers drive.

LiveThe regulator's own warning about its counts5 min▶

NHTSA's Standing General Order requires named manufacturers and operators to report crashes involving automated driving systems (SAE Levels 3 to 5) when the system was in use at any time within 30 seconds of the crash and the crash met damage or injury criteria. Level 2 systems are reportable only for a struck vulnerable road user, a fatality, an air bag deployment or a hospital transport. The page says it plainly: "crash reporting criteria are stricter for ADS".

Then the sentence every leader should be able to quote. The crash counts "have not" been "normalized or adjusted by any measure of exposure, including operational design domains or vehicle miles traveled". Reporting entities are not required to report vehicles operated or distances travelled. The page also lists what else limits the data: reports may be incomplete or unverified, one crash may have several reports, ADS and Level 2 were sometimes misclassified in earlier versions, and telemetry differs by entity.

Real world

A count of reports is a numerator with no denominator, published by the regulator with that warning attached. A fleet that drives ten times the miles can file more reports and be the safer fleet. Ranking companies by report count reads a numerator as if it were a rate, and the regulator has told you in writing not to.

LiveSame drivers, different miles: rural and urban5 min▶

NHTSA's summary of 2022 US crashes splits the 42,514 deaths by area: 17,283 rural (41 percent) and 25,023 urban (59 percent). FHWA puts the rural share of vehicle miles at 32 percent. Rural areas hold 20 percent of the population, 32 percent of the miles and 41 percent of the deaths.

Divide through and the same national population of drivers dies at about 1.69 per 100 million miles on rural roads and about 1.15 on urban ones (derived, approximate because the 32 percent share is rounded). The national 1.33 is a blend of the two, weighted by where people happen to drive.

Real world

This is the exposure mismatch with nothing autonomous in it: the drivers did not change, the roads did, and the rate moved by half again. Any fleet that drives only some kinds of road and compares itself with the all-roads 1.33 or 185.55 is comparing two different blends. Ask for the comparison on the same kind of miles.

LiveThe match, worked, and the fleet where it flips the verdict5 min▶

Lanehart's constructed log has 180 crashes over 150 million miles: 120.00 per 100 million, with an exact 95 percent upper bound of 135.79, comfortably below the human 185.55. But 55 percent of those miles are highway, where Lanehart crashes 69.09 per 100 million; the constructed human mix is only 35 percent highway and carries more urban, night and rain driving. Re-weight Lanehart's own context rates by the human mix and the rate becomes 147.74, upper bound 169.04. Still below 185.55, with a much narrower margin.

Now take the same constructed fleet after only 60 million miles. On its own mix: 82 crashes, 136.67 per 100 million, upper bound 164.22, "proven". Exposure-matched: 168.23, upper bound 205.62, not proven. The thin night and rain miles carry heavy weight in the human mix, and with few miles there the bound is wide. The match did not just shave the margin; it flipped the verdict.

Notice one more line in the figure: in rain, Lanehart's constructed rate of 333.33 is above the constructed human rain rate of 295.40. A single blended rate hid a context where the fleet is worse.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: is the numerator comparable?4 min▶

Matching the miles does not settle the numerator. The 185.55 human baseline is police-reported crashes divided by all vehicle miles (derived from NHTSA 2022), and police reports undercount crashes; RAND's report keeps separate "reported" and "estimated total" rates for that reason. A fleet with full telemetry records minor bumps no human would report.

One side says the fleet is therefore penalised and the true comparison is kinder to it. The other says telemetry fleets also choose what counts as a crash, and that a reporting rule written for one population cannot be assumed to transfer. Both are reasonable. A leader's move is to ask which rule produced each numerator and to write the answer next to the comparison, not to pick a side.

The exposure-matched bound uses the gamma interval for directly standardised rates from Fay and Feuer (1997), reported; practitioner session 7 builds it.

Part 2 · covers disengagements as a metric

A takeover count measures the policy as much as the car

10 min live

California's regulation defines a disengagement as a deactivation of the autonomous mode when a failure of the technology is detected, or when safe operation requires the test driver to take manual control. The second half is a judgement. Who takes over, and how early, is set by the operator's takeover policy. So a fleet can halve its disengagements by changing a rule for its safety drivers, with no change to the car at all.

LiveHow the constructed anti-lever works4 min▶

In the constructed model, Lanehart's supervised miles produce 2,000 conflicts per 100 million miles, each with a severity between 0 and 1. A safety driver takes over when severity exceeds the policy's threshold: 0.50 for cautious, 0.90 for standard, 0.985 for loose. A conflict above 0.97 that is not taken over becomes a crash with probability 0.25.

Cautious and standard both catch everything above 0.97, so both leave the true crash rate at 192.24 per 100 million; cautious simply logs five times the disengagements. Loose lets the band from 0.97 to 0.985 through, adds 21 crashes and lifts the true rate to 206.24, while posting by far the best disengagement score. Score the fleet on disengagements and the score picks the worst policy.

Real world

California's regulation (13 CCR 227.50, as of the 2024 text) asks for monthly autonomous miles on public roads, the circumstances of each disengagement, the location type (interstate, freeway, highway, rural road, street, parking facility) and who initiated it: the technology, the test driver, a remote operator or a passenger. That is a rich record of when control changed hands. It is not a crash rate, and the "who initiated" column is a reminder that a human decision sits inside many of the rows.

LiveWhat disengagement data is good for3 min▶

Read inside one fleet under one fixed policy, disengagements are useful: where they cluster, which location types, which conditions, who initiated. They point the data team at the logs worth mining (practitioner session 3). Across fleets, or across a policy change, a count per mile mostly measures the policy.

The question to ask is simple: did the takeover rule change during the period? If yes, the trend is about the rule until shown otherwise.

Self-studyWhere serious people disagree: disengagement as a metric3 min▶

Some treat disengagements per mile as the most comparable public number available, because the definition is in regulation and the miles come with it. Critics call the definition open to interpretation (reported, trade press) and point out that operators choose test routes and takeover rules. A 2025 proposal from the state regulator would amend the article that holds this section; whether it is adopted is not confirmed here, which is why this page quotes the 2024 text.

The constructed anti-lever does not settle the debate. It shows only that the number can move in the wrong direction for safety, which is enough reason never to read it alone.

Part 3 · build-along

Five gates for any safety claim

17 min hands on

No code. The checklist is five questions in a fixed order, and a claim stops at the first gate it cannot pass. Run Lanehart's constructed claims through the widget first, then take one claim of your own through the same gates on paper.

Live Pick a constructed Lanehart claim. Each gate shows what the claim answers; the first gate it fails is where the conversation stops.

...

Every Lanehart number is constructed (seed 1016) and taken from the engine's canon run. Only 185.55 (derived from NHTSA 2022) and 1.33 are real.

Run all five constructed claims through the widget

Note the gate each one stops at. The raw count stops at gate 2, the own-mix rate at gate 3, the 60 million mile claim at gate 4, the disengagement claim at gate 1, and the matched claim reaches gate 5.

Write one claim of your own in one sentence

From a report, a slide or a press line about driving automation. Underline the number, the unit and the comparison word.

Gate 1: what was counted, and could a policy move it?

Crashes, reported crashes, injuries, deaths or takeovers? Under whose reporting rule? If a decision by the operator can change the count without changing what happens on the road, write "policy metric" and stop.

Gates 2 and 3: how many miles, and which?

If there are no miles, write "not comparable" and stop. If there are, ask for the mix by road type, time of day and weather, and whether the comparison rate comes from the same mix. If not, the claim needs re-weighting before it can be read.

Gates 4 and 5: how sure, and what was never seen?

Ask for the upper bound, not the point (leader session 3). Then ask which rare situations the miles did not include and how they were tested instead (leader session 2). Write down the first gate your claim fails.

The habit to keep. Before you react to a safety number, say its fraction out loud: "this many of these events, over this many miles of this kind, compared with that many over that kind". If you cannot fill every blank, the number is not yet a claim. And when the number is a takeover count, ask what the takeover rule was.

Check yourself

Three questions

In 2022, rural roads carried about 32 percent of US vehicle miles and 41 percent of traffic deaths. A fleet drives only on urban streets and compares its death rate with the national 1.33 per 100 million. What is the problem?

The drivers in the national figures did not change between rural and urban roads; the exposure did. A fair comparison uses the same kind of miles. C invents a cause the data does not show.

Lanehart's constructed crash rate is 120.00 per 100 million on its own miles and 147.74 when re-weighted by the human mix of miles. Why does the rate go up?

The crashes and the context rates are unchanged; only the weights moved. Lanehart drives 55 percent highway against 35 percent in the human mix, so its own blend flatters it.

After a new takeover policy, Lanehart's constructed disengagements fall from 0.193 to 0.027 per 100,000 miles. What should a leader conclude?

A disengagement includes the safety driver's decision to take control, so the takeover rule moves the count. Read across a policy change, the trend measures the rule until shown otherwise.
