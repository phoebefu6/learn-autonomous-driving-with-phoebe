Importance sampling for rare scenarios

Session 7 showed that real miles cannot bound a rare failure. A simulator can replay a scenario as often as you like, but if it draws encounters the way the road does, it almost never sees the failure either. Importance sampling draws them the way the failure happens, then gives every hit a weight that undoes the skew. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies four rare scenario classes whose true failure rates are known exactly, so you can watch both samplers miss or hit the truth; every number on the page is computed in your browser.

Part 0

Two thousand runs, one failure

Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot, has a simulator for a scenario its fleet meets about 4,000 times per 100 million miles: a pedestrian steps out from behind a parked van. The car fails 3 times in 1,000 such encounters, a true rate of 12 crashes per 100 million miles. The team ran 2,000 simulated encounters and saw one failure, so they reported 2.00. The number was wrong by a factor of six, and nothing in the output said so. The same 2,000 runs, drawn differently, give 11.52.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A picture of why plain Monte Carlo starves on a rare failure, and what importance sampling does instead. The weight formula, worked on one draw. The proof, in one line, that the reweighted estimate is unbiased, and the self-check that catches it when the weight is forgotten. And numbers you produced: on Lanehart's four constructed rare classes, at 2,000 encounters per class over 200 runs, the spread of the total is 7.251 for plain Monte Carlo and 1.404 for importance sampling, 5.2 times tighter, with the importance-sampled mean at 46.05 against a true 46.

LiveThe four constructed rare classes4 min▶

Each class is an encounter rate times a failure probability per encounter, both constructed, so the truth is known exactly:

  • Pedestrian steps out from behind a parked van: 4,000 encounters per 100M miles x 0.003 = 12.
  • Wrong-way vehicle on a divided road: 300 x 0.02 = 6.
  • Debris in lane at highway speed: 2,500 x 0.004 = 10.
  • Emergency vehicle crossing a junction: 1,200 x 0.015 = 18.

The tail adds 46 crashes per 100 million miles. Real miles hand you the encounter rate; the failure probability per encounter is what a simulator has to estimate, and it is the small number that breaks plain sampling.

Real world

RAND's "Driving to Safety" (Kalra and Paddock 2016) concluded that developers "cannot simply drive" their way to safety and that simulation and scenario testing are needed alongside road miles. This session is about making those simulated scenarios count: a simulator that samples like the road inherits the road's starvation.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Severity x. In the constructed model, each simulated encounter draws a severity. The car fails when the severity passes a threshold t.
  • Nominal distribution f. How severities occur on the road. Here Exp(1), so P(x > t) = e^-t, and t = -ln(p) makes that exactly p.
  • Proposal distribution g. The skewed sampler. Here Exp with mean t, which puts the threshold at the proposal's mean.
  • Weight w. f(x) / g(x): how much more likely the draw was on the road than in the sampler.
  • Budget. Simulated encounters per class. Both samplers get the same budget, so the comparison is fair.
  • Constructed. The severity model, the thresholds and the classes are built for teaching; your simulator's will differ.
Part 1 · covers plain Monte Carlo against importance sampling at equal budget

Same budget, two samplers

15 min live

Plain Monte Carlo draws each simulated encounter the way the road would, then counts failures. With p = 0.003, 2,000 draws expect 6 failures and saw 1. Importance sampling draws from a distribution centred on the failure threshold, so about 37 percent of draws are failures, and each one is counted at its weight instead of at 1.

LiveWhy plain Monte Carlo starves5 min▶

A plain estimate of p is hits divided by draws. With p = 0.003 and 2,000 draws, the expected count is 6, and a Poisson count with mean 6 lands on 1 or 2 often enough to matter. Each single failure moves the estimate by 1 / 2,000 of an encounter, which is 2.00 crashes per 100 million miles for this class. The estimate can only take a few coarse values, and when it lands on zero it says the scenario is safe.

That is the same starvation session 7 found on real miles, moved into the simulator. A simulator that copies the road's distribution copies its rarity too.

Self-studyWhy the gain is largest on the rarest class4 min▶

The relative error of a plain estimate grows like 1 / sqrt(n p): the smaller p, the more draws it takes to see the event at all. The wrong-way class has p = 0.02, the commonest failure here, and plain Monte Carlo already saw it 42 times; its two estimates are close (6.30 against 6.42). The occluded pedestrian has p = 0.003 and plain sampling saw it once. Importance sampling keeps the hit share near 37 percent whatever p is, so its relative error stays roughly flat as the event gets rarer. That is the property you want for the tail.

Part 2 · covers the weight formula and unbiasedness

The weight, and why the answer stays honest

10 min live

Skewing the sampler toward failure would inflate the rate if each hit still counted as one. The weight undoes the skew draw by draw: it is the ratio of how likely that severity is on the road to how likely it was in the sampler. Deep in the tail, where the sampler oversamples most, the weight is smallest.

LiveThe weight, worked on one draw4 min▶

For the occluded pedestrian, p = 0.003, so t = -ln(0.003) = 5.81. A sampled severity of exactly 5.81 has road likelihood e^-5.81 and sampler likelihood (1/5.81) e^-1. Their ratio is 5.81 x 0.003 x e, about 0.047: on the road that draw is about twenty times rarer than the sampler made it, so it counts as 0.047 of a failure. A draw at 8 counts 0.0077; at 10, 0.0015. A draw below the threshold is a miss and counts 0, whatever its weight.

The estimate is the sum of the weights on hits, divided by the number of draws. The engine's line is short enough to read: w = t * Math.exp(-x + x / t), then add w when x > t.

LiveUnbiasedness in one line3 min▶

Average w(x) times "x is a failure" over the sampler g. Because w = f / g, the g cancels: the average is the integral of f over the failure region, which is p exactly. So the reweighted estimate is right on average for any proposal that puts some probability everywhere the road does. Plain Monte Carlo is also unbiased: its 200-run mean is 45.68, close to 46. Unbiasedness is not the gain; spread is. A single plain run can land anywhere from a missed class to a doubled one, while the importance-sampled runs cluster at 46.05.

The engine checks both halves on every page load: the importance-sampled mean must sit within 6 percent of the true rate over 300 replicates, and the weights must average 1 over the proposal. Plant the dropped-weight fault with AV.selfTest("isNoWeight") in the console and the unbiasedness check fails.

Real world

Zhao, Lam, Peng and colleagues (IEEE Transactions on Intelligent Transportation Systems, 2017) applied this idea to lane-change cut-ins built from naturalistic driving data: they skewed the statistics toward riskier behaviour, used the cross-entropy method to choose the skew, and reweighted results to real-world rates. The authors report an acceleration of 2,000 to 20,000 times: about 1,000 simulated miles exposing the vehicle to challenges that take 2 to 20 million real miles.

Self-studyWhen importance sampling makes things worse4 min▶
  • A proposal that misses part of the failure region. If g puts no probability where failures happen on the road, those failures are never drawn and no weight can recover them. The estimate is then biased low, quietly. Two different failure modes in one scenario need a proposal that reaches both.
  • A proposal skewed too far. A few draws land where g is tiny and f is not; their weights are huge and dominate the sum. The estimate is still unbiased, but its variance can exceed plain Monte Carlo's. Watch the largest weight and the mean weight (it should be near 1).
  • The simulator itself. Importance sampling is unbiased for the simulator's model of the scenario, not for the road. Whether the simulated failure probability matches a real one is the sim-to-real question of session 6, and it stays open: no amount of reweighting closes it.
Self-studyFrom a rate to a bound, and into the bench3 min▶

Each class's estimate comes with a standard error from the spread of its weighted terms. The engine sums the four variances, takes the square root, and adds 1.645 standard errors for a one-sided 95 percent upper bound: tail 44.50, SE 1.321, upper 46.67 at the canon settings. In session 10 that tail is added to the exposure-matched crash rate, 147.74, giving 192.24 with an upper bound of 215.71, above the human 185.55 derived from NHTSA 2022. Without the importance-sampled tail the same fleet looks proven better. Session 10's break button shows exactly that.

Part 3 · build-along

Build both samplers and race them

17 min hands on

Reproduce the canon run in the browser console, then rebuild both samplers in numpy and race them over 200 runs. numpy's random numbers are not the engine's, so your numbers will differ from the canon in the decimals; the pattern will not.

Reproduce the canon class in the console

Open the browser console on this page. The engine is loaded as AV. This is the exact call the bench makes for the occluded class at seed 1016: it should report 1 plain hit, 743 importance-sampled hits and a mean weight near 1. Multiply is by 4,000 and you get the canon 11.52.

consolevar r = AV.rareEstimate(AV.RARE[0], 2000, AV.mulberry32(1016 * 31 + 1000)); [r.mcHits, r.isHits, r.wMean, r.is * 4000]

Rebuild one class in numpy

Plain Monte Carlo counts draws past the threshold. Importance sampling draws from Exp(mean t), weights each draw and averages the weights on hits. Your three numbers will differ from the engine's; the reweighted one should sit near the true 12 and the mean weight near 1.

pythonimport numpy as np rng = np.random.default_rng(1016) enc, p, n = 4000, 0.003, 2000 t = -np.log(p) x = rng.exponential(1.0, n) # plain: as on the road mc = (x > t).mean() y = rng.exponential(t, n) # skewed toward failure w = t * np.exp(-y + y / t) est = np.mean(w * (y > t)) enc * mc, enc * est, w.mean()

Break it on purpose: forget the weight

Count the skewed hits as if each were one failure. The answer is roughly 37 percent of 4,000 encounters, a rate in the thousands. The engine can plant the same fault: AV.selfTest("isNoWeight") in the console returns a failed unbiasedness check. Seeing it once makes the weight unforgettable.

pythonenc * (y > t).mean()

All four classes, 200 runs, race the spread

Each run estimates the whole tail both ways at the same budget. Compare means and standard deviations. Expect both means near 46 and the importance-sampled SD several times smaller; the engine's canon is 7.251 against 1.404, and your numbers will differ.

pythonclasses = [(4000, 0.003), (300, 0.02), (2500, 0.004), (1200, 0.015)] def one_run(rng, n): m = s = 0.0 for enc, p in classes: t = -np.log(p) m += enc * (rng.exponential(1.0, n) > t).mean() y = rng.exponential(t, n) s += enc * np.mean(t * np.exp(-y + y / t) * (y > t)) return m, s runs = np.array([one_run(rng, 2000) for _ in range(200)]) runs.mean(axis=0), runs.std(axis=0, ddof=1)

Change the budget and read the ratio

Rerun with n = 500 and n = 8,000. Both SDs shrink as the budget grows, roughly halving for each fourfold increase; the ratio between the methods barely moves. Count how often a plain run sees zero failures in the occluded class at n = 500. That count is how a simulated scenario gets reported as safe.

Write the estimator down

One line per class: the nominal distribution, the proposal, the weight, the budget, and the mean weight you observed. A rare-scenario rate without its proposal and weight written next to it cannot be checked by anyone else.

The habit to keep. Whenever a simulated scenario reports zero or a handful of failures, ask how many draws landed in the failure region, not how many draws ran. If the answer is a handful, skew the sampler toward the failure, weight every hit by f over g, and check that the weights average 1 before you trust the rate.

Check yourself

Three questions

Plain Monte Carlo with 2,000 encounters saw 1 failure in the occluded pedestrian class and reported 2.00 per 100 million miles; the truth is 12. What went wrong?

Plain Monte Carlo is unbiased (its 200-run mean is 45.68 against 46). Its problem is spread: with so few expected hits, any single run can be far off, including zero.

A colleague skews the simulator toward risky cut-ins and reports the share of failures among the skewed runs as the real-world failure rate. What is missing?

Without weights the estimate converges to the proposal's failure share, about 37 percent here, not to p. More runs make that wrong number more precise.

The importance-sampled rare-scenario rate is unbiased. What does that guarantee about Lanehart's real-world tail risk?

Reweighting corrects the sampler, not the simulator. The sim-to-real gap (session 6) sits underneath every simulated rate.
