The long tail: where the work actually is

Most driving is easy, and a fleet gets very good at it very fast. What decides whether a self-driving car is safe is a list of situations it meets rarely and sometimes fails: a pedestrian stepping out from behind a parked van, a car coming the wrong way, debris at highway speed. Each one is a rate, how often you meet it times how often you fail it, and the fleet's own logs almost never contain enough of the failures to read that rate. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, carries four such situations; every Lanehart number is generated, not observed.

Part 0

Lanehart, the week the dashboard went green

Lanehart's analyst shipped the safety dashboard from session 1. On the fleet's own crash count, matched to the mix of roads people drive, Lanehart comes out at 147.74 crashes per 100 million miles with an upper bound of 169.04, under the derived human baseline of 185.55. The safety lead does not celebrate. She asks one question: "What about the things we almost never see?" Add four rare situations, estimated properly, and the total becomes 192.24, worse than the human baseline. All of it is constructed; the shape of the surprise is not.

★ What you walk out with tonight. A way to write any rare danger as two numbers multiplied: how often the fleet meets it per 100 million miles, and how often it fails when it does. Why that product, not how alarming a situation sounds, is what to rank by. Why a fleet's logged miles hold too few failures to estimate those rates, and what researchers do instead. And a tail ledger you build for your own list of rare situations, which tells you how many miles you would have to drive to see each one fail once.

LiveWhy the green dashboard was not wrong, just incomplete3 min▶

The crash count on the dashboard is honest. It counts what happened on 150 million constructed miles. The problem is what those miles were made of: hour after hour of ordinary driving, where the car is good, and only a thin sprinkle of the rare situations, where it is not. A rate built on that mix measures the ordinary miles very precisely and the rare ones barely at all.

In the constructed run, the four rare situations add 44.50 crashes per 100 million miles when estimated with a method built for rare events (true value 46, known because Lanehart is constructed). Estimated the plain way, from the miles as driven, the same four add 34.95. Leave them out of the evaluation entirely and the dashboard says "proven better" when the truth is worse. Practitioner session 4 calls that the break button.

One caution on the baseline itself: whether a telemetry-rich fleet's crash count can be compared like for like with police-reported human crashes is contested, because a fleet records minor bumps that people never report. The 185.55 is used here as the benchmark the constructed bench uses, not as a settled comparison.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Long tail. The many situations that are each rare but together decide safety. No single one is common; their sum is not small.
  • Encounter rate. How often the fleet meets a situation, per 100 million miles of the driving it actually does.
  • Failure probability. Given an encounter, the chance it ends in a crash. A property of the car's perception, prediction and planning.
  • Tail rate. Encounter rate times failure probability: failures per 100 million miles from that situation.
  • Plain sampling (Monte Carlo). Testing situations in the proportions the road serves them. Fair on average, thin on rare failures.
  • Importance sampling. Testing the hard versions more often on purpose, then weighting each result back to how often it happens on the road. Practitioner session 8.
Part 1 · covers the tail as encounter rate times failure probability

The tail is a product, not a list of scary stories

15 min live

Every rare danger has two parts. The first is exposure: how often the fleet drives into it. The second is weakness: given that it does, how often the car gets it wrong. Multiply them and you get failures per 100 million miles, the same unit as the crash rate on the dashboard. That shared unit is what lets a leader add the tail to the rest of the safety picture, and rank where the work should go.

LiveTwo kinds of rare, one unit5 min▶

Look at the first two rows. The wrong-way vehicle is the scarier story: Lanehart meets one only 300 times per 100 million miles, and fails 2 percent of those encounters, the highest failure probability on the list. The pedestrian behind the parked van is mundane by comparison: met 4,000 times, failed 0.3 percent of the time. Multiply, and the mundane one contributes 12 failures per 100 million miles, twice the wrong-way vehicle's 6.

The largest contribution comes from neither extreme. The emergency vehicle crossing a junction is middling on both factors, 1,200 encounters at 1.5 percent, and gives 18, almost two fifths of the tail. A team that ranked its work by the most dramatic scenario, or by the most frequent one, would start in the wrong place. All four classes and their numbers are constructed, so the truth is knowable; the habit of multiplying before ranking carries over to any real fleet.

Real world

The same shape shows up in real human driving. NHTSA's 2022 summary counts an estimated 5,930,496 police-reported crashes and 42,514 deaths over the same 3,196,191 million vehicle miles. That is about 185.55 reported crashes per 100 million miles, about one every 539,000 miles, but one death every 75.2 million (derived from NHTSA 2022). The worse the outcome, the rarer it is, and the more miles it takes before the data holds even one.

LiveTwo levers, owned by different teams4 min▶

Meet it less often. Encounter rate depends on where and when the fleet drives: its operational design domain, its routes, the hours it runs. Cutting a junction-heavy district out of a route lowers the emergency-vehicle encounter rate without touching the software. It also shrinks the domain, which every claim then has to state.

Fail it less often. Failure probability belongs to perception, prediction and planning. Halving the 0.015 for the emergency vehicle takes that row from 18 to 9, and the tail from 46 to 37 (constructed arithmetic). That is engineering work, and it needs evidence that the new failure probability is real, which is where Part 2 begins.

A leader's question for any rare-situation plan: which lever does this pull, and how will we know it moved?

Self-studyWhy "rare" is not a single property4 min▶

Inside each class the failure probability is not a constant. Lanehart's bench models every encounter with a severity drawn from a common distribution, and a failure happens when severity crosses a threshold set by the class. Most encounters are easy; a few are much harder than average. That is why the same situation can look solved in a thousand tests and still fail on the road: the tests drew the easy end.

It is also why the class list itself is never finished. Every new city, season or road layout adds situations nobody wrote down. ISO 21448 (safety of the intended functionality) is the reported standard concept for exactly this: hazards that arise with no fault at all, from a sensor or a model working as designed and still falling short. Concept only; the standard is paid and was not read in full.

Part 2 · covers why logged miles under-sample the tail

Why the fleet's own logs barely see it

10 min live

A drive log samples the world in the proportions the road serves it. That is perfect for the common case and nearly useless for the rare one, because the thing you need to count is not the encounter but the failure, and failures are rarer still. Take one constructed class and give it 2,000 tries two ways: as the road serves them, and skewed on purpose toward the hard cases.

Live Pick a constructed class and an amount of logged driving. The arithmetic is exact; the rates are Lanehart's, so your numbers will differ on a real fleet.

...

Expected failures = rate per 100M x miles / 100M. The chance of logging none is e to the minus expected failures (a Poisson count). Constructed rates, real maths.
LiveFair on average, useless one class at a time4 min▶

Plain sampling is not biased. Over 200 constructed replicates of the whole tail, its average comes out at 45.68 against a true 46. The trouble is the spread. With 2,000 tries per class, the plain estimate of the tail swings with a standard deviation of 7.251; the skewed-and-reweighted estimate, 1.404, about five times tighter. At 500 tries per class, plain sampling saw zero failures in at least one class in 35 percent of the runs.

Read zero failures in a class as what it is: not enough data, not a solved problem. A dashboard that reports "0 wrong-way failures this quarter" may simply be reporting that the fleet met too few wrong-way vehicles to fail one. Try it in the widget above: 10 million miles with the wrong-way class gives better-than-even odds of logging no failure at all.

Real world

Zhao, Lam, Peng and colleagues (2017, IEEE T-ITS) modelled lane-change cut-ins from naturalistic driving data collected in the University of Michigan Safety Pilot Model Deployment, skewed the cut-in statistics toward riskier behaviour, picked the skew with the cross-entropy method, and reweighted results back to real-world rates. The authors report an acceleration of 2,000 to 20,000 times: about 1,000 simulated miles exposing the vehicle to challenges that take 2 to 20 million real miles to meet.

LiveRAND's conclusion, and what it does not say4 min▶

Kalra and Paddock (2016), in RAND's "Driving to Safety", work through how many miles a fleet would need to drive to show it is safer than people, and conclude that developers "cannot simply drive" their way to safety: test driving alone cannot provide sufficient evidence for fatalities and injuries, and other methods are needed, naming simulation, scenario testing and staged deployment among them.

Read it carefully. It is a statement about evidence, not about whether any car is safe. It does not say driving is useless; logs are where the encounter rates come from, and where new tail classes are discovered. It says the failure probabilities for rare classes have to be measured somewhere the hard cases can be served on purpose. Leader session 3 does RAND's arithmetic for the headline fatality rate.

Self-studyThe catch in skewed testing4 min▶

Skewing and reweighting only works if two things hold. The encounter rate has to come from real driving, because the reweighting multiplies by it. And the simulated encounter has to behave like the real one; a pedestrian model that steps out more politely than people do will report a failure probability that is too low, however many tries it runs. Practitioner session 6 is that gap between simulation and real miles.

So the honest picture is a division of labour. Fleet logs supply exposure and the catalogue of situations. Simulation and scenario testing, weighted back to exposure, supply the failure probabilities. A safety claim that uses only one of the two is missing half its evidence.

Part 3 · build-along

Build a tail ledger for your own list

17 min hands on

No code. Take the ten rare and dangerous situations you listed after session 1 and turn each into a row: how often you would meet it, how often you would fail it, the product, and how many miles you would drive to see one failure. The figure shows the last column for Lanehart's four constructed classes. It is the column that ends the argument about "just drive more".

Write your ten situations as rows

Use the list from session 1's homework. One row each, in plain words, specific enough that two people would agree whether a given moment of driving counts: "a cyclist leaving a parked-car gap at dusk", not "cyclists".

Guess the encounter rate, as a range

Per 100 million miles of the driving your fleet actually does. You will not know; write a low and a high and where the number would come from (logs, a road survey, a map layer). If nobody could ever measure it, mark the row "exposure unknown". That mark is a finding.

Guess the failure probability, as a range

Per encounter. Write where the evidence would come from: logged near misses, simulation, a closed-track test. A range that spans a factor of ten is normal at this stage. Say so rather than picking the middle.

Multiply, then sort by the product

Low times low and high times high give the row's range in failures per 100 million miles. Sort on the high end. Compare the top three with the three you would have picked by instinct before multiplying; note any that moved.

Divide 100 million by the product

That is the miles of driving per one logged failure, the figure's column. Put next to it the miles your fleet logs in a year, if you know it. Every row where the first number is larger than the second cannot be measured from logs alone.

Write how each row will be tested instead

For those rows: mined from logs for near misses (practitioner session 3), simulated and weighted back (practitioner sessions 6 and 8), or not yet tested at all. The last label is the most useful thing on the page for a funding conversation.

The habit to keep. When someone names a rare danger, ask for two numbers, not one: how often we meet it, and how often we fail it. Then ask how many miles it would take to see one failure. If the answer is more than the fleet drives, the evidence has to come from somewhere other than the logs, and the plan should say where.

Check yourself

Three questions

In the constructed Lanehart list, a wrong-way vehicle is met 300 times per 100 million miles and fails 2 percent of the time. A pedestrian behind a parked van is met 4,000 times and fails 0.3 percent of the time. Which adds more to the tail rate?

The tail rate is a product. A high failure probability on a situation the fleet rarely meets can contribute less than a low one on a situation it meets all the time. Rank by the product, not by either factor.

Testing the van class the way the road serves it, 2,000 tries gave 1 failure and an estimate of 2.00 per 100 million miles. The constructed truth is 12. What is the right reading?

Over many constructed runs plain sampling averages close to the truth (45.68 against 46 for the whole tail), so C is wrong. Its spread is the problem: about five times wider than the skewed-and-reweighted estimate at the same budget. B reads noise as good news.

Lanehart's exposure-matched crash rate is 147.74 per 100 million miles, upper bound 169.04, under the 185.55 human baseline. With the four rare classes added, the total is 192.24. What happens if the rare classes are left out of the evaluation?

Each class is rare, but together they add about 46 per 100 million miles, enough to flip the verdict. Dropping them does not make the claim cautious; it makes it wrong in the comfortable direction. Practitioner session 4 is about keeping those slices in.

Before session 3

Homework

Twenty minutes, on your ledger and one guess.

Ask for one real pair of numbers

Pick the top row of your ledger and ask the team closest to it for their best estimate of each factor and where it comes from. Note which of the two nobody can answer; that is usually the encounter rate.

Read RAND's summary

Kalra and Paddock (2016), "Driving to Safety", RAND RR-1478, is a free research report. Read the summary and the conclusion, and underline the one sentence you would quote to a board.

Commit to a number

US drivers had 1.33 deaths per 100 million miles in 2022. Write down how many miles with zero deaths you think a fleet needs before it can claim, at 95 percent confidence, to beat that. Leader session 3 checks it.

Cheat sheet

The one sentence. Safety is decided by situations a fleet meets rarely and sometimes fails, and its own logs barely contain the failures.
The product. Tail rate = encounters per 100M miles x failure probability per encounter.
Rank by the product. Not by how scary it sounds, not by how often it happens.
Two levers. Meet it less (route, domain) or fail it less (perception, prediction, planning).
Miles per failure. 100 million divided by the rate. Bigger than your yearly miles means logs alone cannot measure it.
Zero is not safe. No failures in a class usually means too few encounters, not a solved problem.
Skew and reweight. Serve the hard cases on purpose, then weight back to road rates; exposure still comes from real driving.
RAND. Developers "cannot simply drive" their way to safety; test driving alone is not sufficient evidence.
