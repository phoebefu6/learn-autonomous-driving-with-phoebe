Rare-event statistics

When the event you care about almost never happens, the count you observe is usually zero, and zero is not a rate. It is a statement about how many miles you have driven. Tonight turns a count and its miles into an exact upper bound, finds the miles a zero has to cover before it means anything, and reweights a fleet's crashes so they are compared on the same roads as the human baseline. Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, supplies the counts; the human rates are NHTSA's, and every bound on the page is computed in your browser.

Part 0

Lanehart's dashboard says zero

Lanehart, a constructed robotaxi pilot fleet in a fictional city, has driven 150 million miles and its dashboard shows 0 fatal crashes, rate 0.00. The product lead wants to write "safer than human drivers", since the human rate is 1.33 deaths per 100 million miles. The data scientist's job is to say what those 150 million miles can and cannot carry. The answer is a single upper bound, and on these miles it is 2.00, which is above 1.33. Nothing has been proven yet, in either direction.

LiveThree numbers, one sentence each3 min▶

The count. How many events happened: 0 fatal crashes, or 180 crashes of any severity, in Lanehart's constructed 150 million miles.

The exposure. The miles over which they could have happened. A rate is a count divided by exposure, and with rare events the exposure carries almost all the information.

The bound. The largest true rate still consistent with what you saw, at a stated confidence. A safety claim lives or dies on the bound, never on the point estimate, because the point estimate of a zero count is zero for any number of miles.

Self-studyThe words that keep coming back3 min▶
  • Poisson count. The model for events that happen independently at a steady rate over exposure. The mean is rate times miles.
  • One-sided upper bound. A number U such that, if the true mean were above U, a count this low would happen less than 5% of the time (at 95%).
  • Exact, or Garwood, bound. The upper bound computed from the Poisson distribution itself, not a normal approximation. For k events: the chi-square quantile at C with 2k + 2 degrees of freedom, halved.
  • Rule of three. With zero events, the 95% upper bound is about 3 events, so about 3 / n as a rate.
  • Exposure match, or direct standardisation. Reweighting per-context rates by a reference mix of miles, here the human one.
  • Constructed. Lanehart's miles, contexts and counts come from a seeded generator, not a fleet.
Part 1 · covers the exact Poisson bound and the rule of three

Zero is a bound, not a rate

15 min live

Ask the question backwards. Suppose the true expected number of fatal crashes in Lanehart's 150 million miles were some mean. How likely would it be to see none? For a small mean, quite likely. As the mean grows, a clean record becomes rarer. The upper bound is the mean at which seeing zero has dropped to exactly 5%: any larger mean would make the clean record a less than 5% fluke.

LiveReading the bound off the curve5 min▶

For zero events the chance of a clean record is exp(-mean), so the bound solves exp(-U) = 0.05, which gives U = -ln(0.05) = 2.996 expected events. That is the whole calculation. Turn it into a rate by dividing by the exposure: 2.996 events over 150 million miles is 2.00 per 100 million.

For k events the same idea holds with "k or fewer" in place of "none", and the answer is the exact (Garwood) form: U = chi-square quantile(C, 2k + 2) / 2, the gamma quantile with shape k + 1. With 180 crashes of any severity in the same constructed miles, the point rate is 120.00 per 100 million and the 95% upper bound is 135.79. Notice the shape of that: with many events the bound sits close to the point; with zero events the point is meaningless and the bound is everything.

LiveWhy one-sided, and why exact3 min▶

A safety claim says "at most this bad". Only the upper side can sink it, so all 5% of the doubt goes on that side. A two-sided 95% interval puts 2.5% on each side and gives a different number; say which one you used.

The usual normal approximation, point plus 1.645 standard errors, collapses at zero: the standard error of a zero count is zero, so the "bound" is zero too. The RAND report makes the same point in its own terms, noting that with fewer than 30 events an exact interval can be used, citing Ulm (1990). The engine behind every widget here uses the exact form and checks itself on load.

Real world

Kalra and Paddock's RAND report applies exactly this zero-failure calculation to the human fatality rate it used, 1.09 per 100 million miles in 2013. Showing a rate at most that low at 95% confidence takes 275 million failure-free miles, which the report puts at about 12.5 years for 100 vehicles driving 24 hours a day, every day, at 25 mph.

LiveThe rule of three, and where it comes from4 min▶

Hanley and Lippman-Hand (1983) put the shortcut in a medical journal under the title "If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right?": with 0 events in n trials, the 95% upper limit is about 3 / n (reported; we did not read the abstract at source, and the result is derivable). The derivation is the curve above: -ln(0.05) = 2.996, which rounds to 3.

The cousins follow the same formula, -ln(1 - C). At 90% the count is 2.303, at 99% it is 4.605 (derived). For Lanehart's 150 million constructed miles the zero-event bounds are 1.535, 2.00 and 3.070 per 100 million. The shortcut is only for zero; with any events, use the exact form.

Self-studyWhen the Poisson model itself is wrong4 min▶

Every bound here assumes events arrive independently at a steady rate over the miles. Three ways that breaks, none of which the bound can see: crashes that cluster (one software release, one stretch of road, one weather week), a rate that changes over the miles because the system was updated mid-way, and miles that are not alike (a highway mile and a rain mile are different exposures). The last one is fixed in Part 2 by matching exposure. The first two need the miles split by release and period, and a bound per split.

A Bayesian treatment of the same rate, with a prior over it, is owned by learn Bayesian (beta-binomial). The side picked here is the frequentist exact bound over miles of exposure, because that is the form the RAND arithmetic uses.

Part 2 · covers miles needed and the exposure-matched bound

How many miles a zero must cover, and on which roads

10 min live

Flip the bound around. If a fleet sees no fatal crashes at all, its 95% bound is 2.996 divided by its miles, so the bound slides down as the miles grow. The miles needed are where it crosses the human rate. Then a second problem: a fleet's miles are not the human population's miles. Compare on the same mix of roads, or the comparison measures where each one drove.

LiveMiles needed, read off the widget3 min▶
  • 0 events, 95%, 1.33: the bound is 2.00 at 150 million miles and the miles needed are 225.2 million. Set the miles to 225.2 million and the bound sits on the line.
  • Switch to RAND's 1.09: the miles needed rise to 275 million, the report's own figure. The 2022 rate is higher than the 2013 one, so the bar fell; nothing about the cars changed.
  • Switch to 90% or 99%: 173.1 million or 346.3 million. Whoever picks the confidence level moves the finish line, so it is picked before the data, and written down.
  • Now observe 1 event: the miles needed jump, because the bound must now cover "1 or fewer". A single event late in a programme costs more miles than most people expect. Your numbers will differ with every setting; the widget prints them.

RAND goes further: estimating the fatality rate to within 20% takes about 8.8 billion miles, and showing it is 20% better than humans with 80% power takes about 11 billion. Those are precision and superiority questions, which leader session 3 walks through. The bound tonight answers only "is it at most the human rate?".

LiveThe matched rate and its bound4 min▶

Directly standardising is a weighted sum: take Lanehart's rate in each road context and weight it by the human share of miles in that context, 35% highway, 40% urban day, 15% night, 10% rain (a constructed mix). Lanehart's own mix is 55 / 30 / 10 / 5, heavy on highway, where its rate is lowest, so the plain rate of 120.00 flatters it. Matched, it is 147.74.

The bound on a weighted sum of Poisson counts is not the exact Poisson bound any more. Fay and Feuer (1997) give a gamma interval for it (reported for the citation; the method is implemented in the engine and checked by simulation in the reference script, where its one-sided 95% coverage at 60 million miles came out 0.9575 over 2,000 constructed draws). The upper bound is 169.04 at 150 million miles, under the derived human 185.55: proven, with a narrower margin. At 60 million miles the matched rate is 168.23 and the bound 205.62: not proven, because the thin night and rain miles carry big weights and few events. The widget's Lanehart button prints both.

A useful identity, which the engine's self-check tests: weight by the fleet's own mix and the Fay-Feuer bound returns exactly the plain Poisson bound, 135.79. The matched bound only differs because the weights differ.

Real world

NHTSA's 2022 summary gives the mismatch in one line: rural areas held 20% of the population and 32% of the miles but 41% of the deaths. Derived from those shares, the rural fatality rate is about 1.69 per 100 million miles against about 1.15 urban, approximate because the 32% is rounded. Two fleets with the same drivers and different road mixes would post different rates.

Self-studyWhat the matched bound still cannot settle4 min▶

The numerator is not like for like. The 185.55 is police-reported crashes, which undercount all crashes; RAND keeps separate "reported" and "estimated total" rates for that reason. A fleet with full telemetry records more of its minor crashes than a human population reports. Some reviewers read that as making a fleet's crash count look worse than it is; others read the police baseline as the only audited number available. Whether the two are comparable is contested, and the bound does not decide it.

The context list is a choice. Four contexts is a constructed simplification. Real exposure differs by speed, junction density, time of day, weather and more, and each extra stratum thins the miles in every cell, which widens the bound. Matching on more is not free.

The rare tail is missing. Logged miles barely sample the rare scenarios, so a bound on logged crashes says little about them. Practitioner session 8 estimates that tail by importance sampling; the bench in session 10 adds it and the verdict turns.

Part 3 · build-along

Build the bounds in Python and match the canon

17 min hands on

Four small functions, every output checked against a number already on this page. Use a notebook with scipy and numpy. The figure is the plan, and the yellow chip is the bug that matters: the wrong weights still return a tidy number.

The exact upper bound

One line from the chi-square quantile. Check the zero case against -ln(0.05) before trusting it with anything else; both print 2.9957.

pythonimport math import numpy as np from scipy import stats def exact_upper(k, conf=0.95): return stats.chi2.ppf(conf, 2 * k + 2) / 2 exact_upper(0), -math.log(0.05)

Turn counts into rates and miles

Divide by miles, scale to 100 million. You should see 2.00 and 135.79 for Lanehart, then 225.2, 173.1 and 346.3 million miles needed.

pythondef upper_per_100m(k, miles, conf=0.95): return exact_upper(k, conf) / miles * 1e8 def miles_needed(rate_per_100m, k=0, conf=0.95): return exact_upper(k, conf) / (rate_per_100m / 1e8) print(upper_per_100m(0, 150e6), upper_per_100m(180, 150e6)) print([round(miles_needed(1.33, conf=c) / 1e6, 1) for c in (0.90, 0.95, 0.99)])

The Fay-Feuer matched bound

Weights per mile in each context, a weighted sum, and a gamma quantile whose shape and scale carry the largest weight as a correction. Feed it Lanehart's four constructed context counts: 147.74 and 169.04.

pythondef ff_upper(ks, miles, w, conf=0.95): wi = [wj / m for wj, m in zip(w, miles)] y = sum(a * k for a, k in zip(wi, ks)) v = sum(a * a * k for a, k in zip(wi, ks)) wm = max(wi) shape = (y + wm) ** 2 / (v + wm ** 2) scale = (v + wm ** 2) / (y + wm) return y * 1e8, stats.gamma.ppf(conf, shape, scale=scale) * 1e8 share = [0.55, 0.30, 0.10, 0.05] miles = [150e6 * s for s in share] ks = [57, 70, 28, 25] ff_upper(ks, miles, [0.35, 0.40, 0.15, 0.10])

Break it on purpose: the wrong weights

Pass Lanehart's own shares as the weights. You get 120.00 and 135.79, exactly the plain Poisson answer from step 2. No error, no warning, a smaller number: this is the mistake the engine's self-check exists to catch.

pythonff_upper(ks, miles, share)

Check the bound covers the truth

A bound is a claim about repeated draws, so test it that way. Draw constructed counts at 60 million miles from the known true context rates, compute the bound each time, and count how often it covers the true matched rate. It should land near 0.95; the reference script reports 0.9575 over 2,000 draws. Your numbers will differ with your seed.

pythonrng = np.random.default_rng(1016) true = np.array([60, 150, 200, 330]) w = [0.35, 0.40, 0.15, 0.10] m60 = 60e6 * np.array(share) target = float(np.dot(w, true)) covers = sum(ff_upper(rng.poisson(m60 * true / 1e8), m60, w)[1] >= target for _ in range(2000)) target, covers / 2000

Write the claim sentence

One sentence with every part named: the count, the miles, the confidence, which side, the method, the reference mix and the benchmark. "180 crashes in 150 million constructed miles; exposure-matched to the human road mix, 147.74 per 100 million, one-sided 95% Fay-Feuer upper bound 169.04, against 185.55 police-reported crashes derived from NHTSA 2022." Anything shorter hides a choice.

The habit to keep. Never report a rare-event rate without its exposure and its upper bound, and never compare two rates measured on different mixes of roads. Pick the confidence level and the reference mix before you look at the counts, and test the bound against a case where you know the truth.

Check yourself

Three questions

Constructed Lanehart has 0 fatal crashes in 150 million miles. What does a 95% one-sided analysis say against the human 1.33 per 100 million?

With zero events the point estimate is zero at any mileage, so it carries no information. The bound is -ln(0.05) = 2.996 events over the miles, and it reaches 1.33 only at 225.2 million miles.

Lanehart's plain crash rate is 120.00 per 100 million; matched to the human road mix it is 147.74, from the same crashes. Why does it rise?

The counts and per-context rates are unchanged; only the weights moved. The margin lives in the upper bound (169.04), not in the point.

Raising the confidence from 95% to 99% moves the zero-event miles needed from 225.2 million to 346.3 million. Why?

Miles needed are -ln(1 - C) divided by the benchmark rate. The benchmark is fixed; only the count of events the bound allows changes. That is why the confidence level is chosen before the data.
